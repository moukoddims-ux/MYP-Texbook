@SEC | 10.1 | Collecting and organising data | Criterion A · i, ii; Criterion D · i
@LEAD | Before any graph is drawn, data must be collected honestly and organised so that it can be checked. A frequency table is the working document of statistics.
@DEF | Data and frequency
**Data** are observations, measurements or counts. Data are **qualitative** when they describe a category (favourite sport) and **quantitative** when they are numbers (height in cm). Quantitative data are **discrete** if they can be counted (number of siblings) and **continuous** if they can be measured on a scale (time, mass, length).
The **frequency** of a value is the number of times it occurs. A **frequency table** lists each value with its frequency.
@FIG | c10_freq
@KEY | A good table can be checked
Three checks catch almost every error in a frequency table: the frequencies must add to the number of observations; every observation must appear exactly once; and the categories must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive, so that no observation is lost or counted twice — the same requirement as a sound classification.
@METHOD | Building a frequency table
@LISTO
1. Decide whether the data are qualitative or quantitative.
2. For qualitative data, list the categories and use tally marks.
3. For discrete quantitative data, list the possible values in order.
4. For continuous data, choose intervals of equal width and state whether boundaries are included.
5. Total the frequencies and check against the number of observations.

@ERR | Intervals that overlap or leave gaps
Intervals “0–10, 10–20, 20–30” are ambiguous: a value of exactly 10 belongs to two groups. Write “0 ≤ x < 10, 10 ≤ x < 20” or state the convention once. Unequal interval widths also distort a bar chart, because a wider interval contains more data for the same height.

@EX | 10.1.1 | Classifying data
GIVEN: Four sets of data: shoe size, favourite colour, time to run 100 m, number of books read.
ASKED: Classify each as qualitative or quantitative, and quantitative as discrete or continuous.
METHOD: Ask whether the data are categories or numbers; then whether the numbers are counted or measured.
STEPS:
1. Shoe size: quantitative, discrete.
2. Favourite colour: qualitative.
3. Time to run 100 m: quantitative, continuous.
4. Number of books: quantitative, discrete.
ANSWER: discrete, qualitative, continuous, discrete.
CHECK: Shoe sizes are counted in half units and can be listed; times can take any value in a range.

@EX | 10.1.2 | Completing a frequency table
GIVEN: A table of 40 observations with frequencies 7, 12, 9, $x$ and 5 for five categories.
ASKED: Find $x$.
METHOD: Use the total.
STEPS:
1. $7 + 12 + 9 + 5 = 33$.
2. $x = 40 - 33 = 7$.
ANSWER: $x = 7$.
CHECK: $7 + 12 + 9 + 7 + 5 = 40$.

@EX | 10.1.3 | Choosing intervals
GIVEN: 60 masses in kilograms ranging from 41.2 to 68.9.
ASKED: Choose suitable intervals and explain the choice.
METHOD: Aim for five to eight intervals of equal width covering the range.
STEPS:
1. Range: $68.9 - 41.2 = 27.7$ kg.
2. Five intervals of 6 kg from 40 give 40–, 46–, 52–, 58–, 64–, which covers the data.
3. State that each interval includes its lower bound and excludes its upper bound.
ANSWER: Five intervals of width 6 kg starting at 40.
CHECK: The highest value 68.9 falls in the interval 64–70, so no observation is left out.
WATCH: Rounding the range down can leave the largest value without an interval. Always check the extremes.

@SEC | 10.2 | Displaying data: bar charts, dot plots and pictograms | Criterion C · i, ii, iv
@LEAD | A good graph answers the question the data were collected to answer. The wrong choice of graph hides the pattern, or invents one.
@FIG | c10_bar
@FIG | c10_dot
@TABLE | Choosing a display
Display ; Best for ; Avoid when
Frequency table ; exact values and totals ; the shape matters
Bar chart ; comparing categories ; the data are continuous measurements
Dot plot ; small sets of discrete values ; there are hundreds of values
Histogram ; grouped continuous data ; the categories are labels
Pictogram ; a general audience, simple comparisons ; precise comparison is needed
Pie chart ; parts of a single whole ; more than one whole is compared
@KEY | What every graph must have
@LISTU
- A title that says what is being shown.
- Labelled axes with units, or a key for a pictogram.
- A vertical axis that starts at zero for bar charts and histograms.
- Bars of equal width, separated for categories and touching for continuous intervals.
@METHOD | Drawing a bar chart
@LISTO
1. Choose a scale for the vertical axis that starts at zero and fits the largest value.
2. Draw and label the axes, including units.
3. Draw bars of equal width with equal gaps.
4. Add a title and, where useful, the value on each bar.
5. Check that the tallest bar matches the greatest frequency.
@ERR | Bars that touch in a bar chart
In a bar chart the categories are separate, so the bars must not touch. Touching bars signal a histogram, where the horizontal axis is a continuous scale. Mixing the two conventions confuses the reader about whether the data are categories or intervals.

@EX | 10.2.1 | Reading a bar chart
GIVEN: A bar chart of favourite sports for 120 students: football 40, basketball 30, swimming 20, athletics 15, other 15.
ASKED: Find the fraction who chose football or basketball, and the angle each would occupy in a pie chart.
METHOD: Combine the counts, then convert to fractions and to angles of 360°.
STEPS:
1. Football or basketball: $40 + 30 = 70$, which is $\\frac{70}{120} = \\frac{7}{12}$.
2. Football angle: $\\frac{40}{120} \\times 360° = 120°$.
3. Basketball: $\\frac{30}{120} \\times 360° = 90°$.
ANSWER: $\\frac{7}{12}$; 120° and 90°.
CHECK: The five angles total $120 + 90 + 60 + 45 + 45 = 360°$.

@EX | 10.2.2 | Reading a dot plot
GIVEN: A dot plot of the number of books read by 13 students: 1 (one student), 2 (two), 3 (three), 4 (two), 5 (four), 6 (one).
ASKED: State the most common number, the range and the number of students who read more than 4 books.
METHOD: Read the heights of the stacks.
STEPS:
1. The tallest stack is at 5, with four students.
2. Range: $6 - 1 = 5$.
3. More than 4: the 5 and 6 stacks, $4 + 1 = 5$ students.
ANSWER: 5 books; range 5; 5 students.
CHECK: Frequencies sum to $1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 4 + 1 = 13$.

@EX | 10.2.3 | Criticising a display
GIVEN: A bar chart with no vertical scale, three-dimensional bars and an axis starting at 80.
ASKED: Explain three ways it could mislead.
METHOD: Check each convention in turn.
STEPS:
1. No scale: the reader cannot recover the values.
2. Axis from 80: differences are exaggerated.
3. Three-dimensional bars: the front face is proportional, but the volume is not, so the eye overestimates large bars.
ANSWER: Missing scale, truncated axis and misleading perspective.
CHECK: A correct version would show the axis from zero, a labelled scale and flat bars.
WATCH: A graph can be accurate in every number and still mislead through its design. Checking the axes is the reader's responsibility.

@SEC | 10.3 | Averages and range | Criterion A · ii, iii; Criterion C · iii
@LEAD | An average is a single number used to summarise a set of data. There are three common averages, and they answer slightly different questions.
@DEF | Mean, median, mode and range
The **mean** is the total divided by the number of values. The **median** is the middle value when the data are in order. The **mode** is the most frequent value. The **range** is the difference between the largest and smallest values and measures spread, not average.
@KEY | Which average to use
@LISTU
- The mean uses every value, so it reflects the whole data set — and is pulled by extreme values.
- The median ignores the size of extreme values and is more representative when the data are skewed.
- The mode is the only average that works for qualitative data, such as the most popular colour.
- The range is easy to compute but depends on only two values.
@TABLE | Three averages of the same data
Data: 3, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9, 14 ; Value ; Reason
Mean ; 7.14 (to 3 s.f.) ; $50 \\div 7$
Median ; 6 ; fourth of seven ordered values
Mode ; 5 ; occurs twice
Range ; 11 ; $14 - 3$
@METHOD | Calculating each average
@LISTO
1. Order the data before finding the median.
2. For an odd number of values, the median is the middle one; for an even number, it is the mean of the two middle values.
3. For the mean, add all values and divide by how many there are.
4. For the mode, count frequencies; a data set may have no mode or several.
@ERR | Forgetting to order the data
Finding the median of 7, 2, 9, 4, 5 by taking the middle of the written list gives 9, which is the largest value. In order, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, the median is 5. Ordering is not a preliminary step that can be skipped.

@EX | 10.3.1 | Finding all three averages
GIVEN: The data 12, 9, 14, 9, 11, 15, 9, 10.
ASKED: Find the mean, median, mode and range.
METHOD: Order the data, then apply each definition.
STEPS:
1. Ordered: 9, 9, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15.
2. Mean: $89 \\div 8 = 11.125$.
3. Median: mean of the fourth and fifth values, $10$ and $11$, so 10.5.
4. Mode: 9. Range: $15 - 9 = 6$.
ANSWER: mean 11.125, median 10.5, mode 9, range 6.
CHECK: The mean lies between the median and the largest values, as expected with a slight upper tail.

@EX | 10.3.2 | Working backwards from the mean
GIVEN: Five test scores have a mean of 14. Four of them are 12, 15, 13 and 16.
ASKED: Find the fifth score.
METHOD: Use the total implied by the mean.
STEPS:
1. Total $= 5 \\times 14 = 70$.
2. Known total $= 12 + 15 + 13 + 16 = 56$.
3. Fifth score $= 70 - 56 = 14$.
ANSWER: 14.
CHECK: $(12 + 15 + 13 + 16 + 14) \\div 5 = 70 \\div 5 = 14$.

@EX | 10.3.3 | Choosing the better average
GIVEN: Salaries in a small company: 18,000; 19,000; 19,500; 20,000; 21,000; 22,000; 180,000.
ASKED: Decide whether the mean or the median better represents a typical salary.
METHOD: Compare the two and consider the effect of the extreme value.
STEPS:
1. Mean: total $= 299{,}500$, divided by 7 is about 42,786.
2. Median: 20,000.
3. The mean is more than twice the median because one salary is far larger.
ANSWER: The median, 20,000, represents a typical salary better; the mean is distorted by the single extreme value.
CHECK: Six of the seven salaries are below the mean.
WATCH: An average that most of the data lie below is usually the wrong choice to describe “typical”.

@SEC | 10.4 | Grouped data | Criterion A · iii; Criterion B · ii
@LEAD | When data are grouped, the individual values are lost, so averages can only be estimated. A histogram shows the shape, and the midpoint of each interval stands in for the values inside it.
@DEF | Grouped data and the estimated mean
For grouped data, the **midpoint** of each interval represents its values, and the estimated mean is
$\\text{mean} \\approx \\frac{\\sum (\\text{midpoint} \\times \\text{frequency})}{\\sum \\text{frequency}}$
The **modal class** is the interval with the greatest frequency, and the **median class** is the interval containing the middle value.
@FIG | c10_hist
@METHOD | Estimating the mean from a grouped table
@LISTO
1. Find the midpoint of each interval.
2. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency.
3. Add these products, and divide by the total frequency.
4. State that the answer is an estimate, and say why.
@TABLE | Estimated mean of grouped times
Interval ; Midpoint ; Frequency ; Midpoint × frequency
6–10 ; 8 ; 4 ; 32
11–15 ; 13 ; 9 ; 117
16–20 ; 18 ; 14 ; 252
21–25 ; 23 ; 8 ; 184
26–30 ; 28 ; 5 ; 140
Total ; ; 40 ; 725
@P | Estimated mean $= 725 \\div 40 = 18.125$ seconds. The true mean would need the individual times; using midpoints assumes the values in each interval are evenly spread, which is an assumption, not a fact.
@ERR | Using the upper boundary as the midpoint
For the interval 11–15 the midpoint is 13, not 15. Using the boundary overstates every value and inflates the estimated mean. The midpoint is the average of the two boundaries.
@KEY | What grouping hides
Two data sets with the same grouped table can have different true means, medians and ranges. Once the raw values are replaced by intervals, the information is lost for ever: this is why the raw data should be kept, and why a grouped mean is always reported as an estimate.

@EX | 10.4.1 | Estimating the mean
GIVEN: The grouped table above.
ASKED: Estimate the mean and state the modal class.
METHOD: Use midpoints and frequencies.
STEPS:
1. Products: $8 \\times 4 = 32$, $13 \\times 9 = 117$, $18 \\times 14 = 252$, $23 \\times 8 = 184$, $28 \\times 5 = 140$.
2. Total $= 725$; frequency total $= 40$.
3. Estimated mean $= 18.125$.
4. The greatest frequency is 14, in the interval 16–20.
ANSWER: about 18.1 seconds; modal class 16–20.
CHECK: The mean lies inside the modal class, which is usual for a single-peaked distribution.

@EX | 10.4.2 | Finding the median class
GIVEN: Frequencies 4, 9, 14, 8, 5 for five intervals, with a total of 40.
ASKED: State the median class.
METHOD: Find where the 20th and 21st values fall in the cumulative totals.
STEPS:
1. Cumulative: 4, 13, 27, 35, 40.
2. The 20th and 21st values lie in the third interval, since the count reaches 27 there.
ANSWER: The median class is the third interval, 16–20.
CHECK: The interval contains values 14 to 27 in order, which includes both middle positions.

@EX | 10.4.3 | Comparing two classes
GIVEN: Class A: mean 62, range 8. Class B: mean 62, range 30.
ASKED: Compare the two classes.
METHOD: Compare both centre and spread.
STEPS:
1. The means are equal, so the classes performed similarly on average.
2. The ranges differ greatly: Class B's results are much more spread out.
ANSWER: Same average, very different consistency; Class A is more consistent.
CHECK: Two data sets can share an average and differ entirely in spread — which is why range or another measure of spread should be reported with any average.
WATCH: Reporting only the mean conceals differences that matter. A mean without a measure of spread is an incomplete summary.

@SEC | 10.5 | Reading data critically | Criterion C · iii; Criterion D · iii, v
@LEAD | A statistic is a claim about the world, and claims can be checked. Reading critically means asking what was measured, how it was summarised and what the presentation leaves out.
@FIG | c10_mislead
@METHOD | Five questions for any data claim
@LISTO
1. **What exactly was measured, and on whom?** A sample of volunteers is not a random sample.
2. **How many?** A difference of 10% in a sample of 20 may be one person.
3. **Which average was used, and why?** The mean and median can tell different stories.
4. **What is the spread?** An average without a range hides how consistent the data are.
5. **What does the graph do?** Check the axis, the scale, the units and whether the picture is proportional.
@KEY | Correlation is not cause
Two quantities can rise together without one causing the other. Ice-cream sales and drowning incidents both rise in hot weather; the cause is the weather, not the ice cream. A data set can show an association; it cannot, by itself, show that one thing caused another.
@TABLE | Claims and their weaknesses
Claim ; Question to ask ; Likely problem
“9 out of 10 prefer our brand” ; How many were asked, and who? ; Small or self-selected sample
“Average waiting time is 4 minutes” ; Mean or median? ; A few very short waits may pull the mean down
“Sales up 200%” ; From what base? ; A small base makes a large percentage
“Results improved” ; Compared with what, and by how much? ; No comparison group or effect size
@ERR | Reporting a percentage without its base
“60% more students passed” means one thing if 10 passed last year and quite another if 200 did. The base belongs with the percentage — the same principle as in Chapter 5, where the base determined everything.

@EX | 10.5.1 | Interrogating a survey
GIVEN: A school newsletter reports: “90% of students say the canteen is good value. 100 students were surveyed during one lunchtime.”
ASKED: Identify three weaknesses and suggest an improvement.
METHOD: Apply the five questions.
STEPS:
1. Sample: students present at one lunchtime may not represent those who never use the canteen.
2. Wording: “good value” is not defined, so responses may not be comparable.
3. Summary: no information on how many students exist overall, so the size of the sample relative to the school is unknown.
ANSWER: Sampling bias, undefined wording and missing context; a random sample across year groups with a defined question would improve it.
CHECK: A survey answered only by canteen users cannot measure satisfaction among non-users.

@EX | 10.5.2 | Comparing two summaries
GIVEN: Two classes take a test. Class A: mean 65, median 66, range 12. Class B: mean 65, median 58, range 40.
ASKED: Explain what each figure reveals and recommend which summary to report.
METHOD: Compare centre and spread.
STEPS:
1. The means are equal, so the headline figure suggests no difference.
2. The medians differ by 8, showing Class B has more low scores; its range of 40 confirms a wide spread.
3. Reporting the mean alone hides the difference.
ANSWER: Report the median and range as well as the mean; Class A is more consistent and higher in the middle.
CHECK: A class with several very high scores can have a high mean and a low median.

@EX | 10.5.3 | Detecting a misleading graph
GIVEN: A bar chart where bars have equal widths but the vertical axis starts at 40, and the shortest bar is cut off entirely.
ASKED: State what the chart implies and what the data actually show.
METHOD: Read the axes, not the picture.
STEPS:
1. Starting at 40 exaggerates the ratio between bars.
2. A cut-off bar makes a category look like zero when it may be 39.
3. The true differences must be read from the labels, not the heights.
ANSWER: The chart implies large differences; the data may show small ones.
CHECK: Redrawing with an axis from zero gives a proportional picture.
WATCH: A graph with a truncated axis is not necessarily dishonest, but it is misleading unless the break is clearly marked.

@MOVE | G | Reflect and consolidate | Summary, vocabulary, misconceptions and review
@SEC | Review | Consolidating Chapter 10 | Criterion A · i, ii, iii
@H2 | What this chapter established
@LISTU
- Data are qualitative or quantitative; quantitative data are discrete or continuous.
- A frequency table records counts and can be checked by its total.
- Bar charts suit categories, dot plots suit small discrete sets, histograms suit grouped continuous data.
- The mean, median and mode summarise the centre in different ways; the range measures spread.
- Grouped data can only give an estimated mean, using interval midpoints.
- Graphs and statistics can mislead through sampling, choice of average, truncated axes and missing context.
@H2 | Essential vocabulary
@TABLE | Vocabulary for Chapter 10
Term | Meaning in this course
Qualitative data | Data describing a category.
Discrete data | Countable values, usually whole numbers.
Continuous data | Measurements that can take any value in a range.
Frequency | The number of times a value occurs.
Mean | Total divided by the number of values.
Median | The middle value of ordered data.
Mode | The most frequent value.
Range | The difference between the largest and smallest values.
Histogram | A chart of grouped continuous data with touching bars.
Modal class | The interval with the greatest frequency.
@H2 | Common misconceptions
@TABLE | Misconception, correction and evidence
Misconception ; Correction ; Evidence
“The median is the middle of the list as written.” ; Order the data first. ; 9 in 7, 2, 9, 4, 5
“The range is an average.” ; It measures spread. ; 15 is not a centre
“The mean is always the best summary.” ; One extreme value can distort it. ; 7 salaries
“Touching bars are always wrong.” ; Histograms need them. ; continuous intervals
“A bigger bar means more if the axis is cut.” ; Read the scale. ; axis from 40
