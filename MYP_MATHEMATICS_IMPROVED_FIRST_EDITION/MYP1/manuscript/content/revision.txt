@SEC | Extended project | Designing a mathematics trail | Project
@LEAD | This project brings the whole course together. It is designed to be completed over two or three lessons, in groups, and it produces a piece of work that can be assessed against all four criteria.
@APPLY | The brief
@P | Your group will design a **mathematics trail** for visitors to your school or neighbourhood: a route of six stations, each with a mathematical task that uses a measurement, a calculation or a data set from that place. The trail must be usable by a student in the year below without a teacher's help.
@H2 | Stage 1 · Choose and measure
@LISTO
1. Walk the route and choose six stations: for example a staircase, a noticeboard, a sports court, a tree or a window.
2. At each station, take the measurements needed to write a task. Record them with units and note how accurate each measurement is.
3. Photograph or sketch each station, and note one safety point.
@H2 | Stage 2 · Write the tasks
@LISTO
1. Write one task per station. Across the six tasks, cover at least four of: place value and rounding; powers; factors; fractions or percentages; algebra; angle or shape; perimeter, area or volume; sets; data.
2. Give each task a clear instruction, the data needed, and a space for the answer.
3. Write the answers on a separate sheet, with the method for each.
@H2 | Stage 3 · Test and revise
@LISTO
1. Ask another group to attempt two of your tasks. Record what confused them.
2. Revise the wording of any task that was misunderstood, and say what you changed.
3. Check every answer again, including the units.
@H2 | Stage 4 · Present
@LISTO
1. Produce a trail leaflet: a map, the six stations, the tasks and a safety note.
2. Write a short introduction explaining what a visitor will practise.
@TABLE | How the project is assessed
Criterion ; Evidence in the project
A · Knowing and understanding ; Correct mathematics in the tasks and answers, with consistent units.
B · Investigating patterns ; At least one station where the visitor must find a general rule from measurements.
C · Communicating ; The leaflet: clear instructions, labelled diagrams, accurate notation.
D · Applying mathematics ; Real measurements, real places, a usable product, limitations stated.
@CALLOUT | Keep this question for the end: which station taught the visitor the most mathematics, and why?

@SEC | Worked solutions | Twenty-five problems solved in full | Method, reasoning and check
@LEAD | An answer tells you whether you were right; a solution tells you how to think. The twenty-five problems below are chosen because each one is a place where Year 1 students commonly lose marks, and each solution is written in the same five steps: read, plan, work, answer, check.
@H2 | Number and place value
@H3 | 1 · Rounding with a decision
**Question.** Round 4,836 to the nearest hundred.
**Read.** The hundreds digit is 8; the digit that decides is the 3 in the tens place.
**Plan.** Look only at the deciding digit, then choose the nearer hundred.
**Work.** The number lies between 4,800 and 4,900. 4,836 is 36 above 4,800 and 64 below 4,900, so it is nearer 4,800.
**Answer.** 4,800.
**Check.** Rounding to the nearest ten gives 4,840, so the hundreds answer must be a multiple of 100 near it. 4,800 is consistent.
@H3 | 2 · The order of operations with a bracket
**Question.** Work out $48 \div (6 - 2) + 3^{2}$.
**Plan.** Bracket first, then the power, then division, then addition.
**Work.** $6 - 2 = 4$; $3^{2} = 9$; $48 \div 4 = 12$; $12 + 9 = 21$.
**Answer.** 21.
**Check.** A quick estimate: 50 ÷ 4 ≈ 12.5, plus 9 gives about 21.5, and 21 is close to that. Changing the order would give $48 \div (4 + 9) \approx 3.7$, so the brackets are doing real work.
@H3 | 3 · HCF and LCM chosen by context
**Question.** Tiles 24 cm by 18 cm are to cover a square exactly. Find the smallest possible square.
**Plan.** The side of the square must be a common multiple of both tile lengths; the smallest is the LCM.
**Work.** $24 = 2^{3} \times 3$ and $18 = 2 \times 3^{2}$, so LCM $= 2^{3} \times 3^{2} = 72$.
**Answer.** A 72 cm square, using $3 \times 4 = 12$ tiles.
**Check.** $72 \div 24 = 3$ and $72 \div 18 = 4$, both whole numbers, and no smaller number works because 24 and 18 share the factor 6.
@H3 | 4 · Prime factorisation in index form
**Question.** Write 360 as a product of primes.
**Work.** $360 = 36 \times 10 = (2^{2} \times 3^{2}) \times (2 \times 5) = 2^{3} \times 3^{2} \times 5$.
**Check.** $8 \times 9 \times 5 = 360$. Only primes appear, and the factorisation is unique apart from order.
@H2 | Fractions, decimals and percentages
@H3 | 5 · Adding fractions with unlike denominators
**Question.** Work out $\frac{5}{6} - \frac{3}{8}$.
**Plan.** The LCM of 6 and 8 is 24, so both fractions can be written in twenty-fourths.
**Work.** $\frac{5}{6} = \frac{20}{24}$ and $\frac{3}{8} = \frac{9}{24}$; the difference is $\frac{11}{24}$.
**Answer.** $\frac{11}{24}$, which is already in simplest form because 11 is prime and does not divide 24.
**Check.** $\frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833$ and $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$, so the difference is about $0.458$, and $\frac{11}{24} \approx 0.458$. The decimal check agrees with the fraction.
@H3 | 6 · A recurring decimal as a fraction
**Question.** Write $0.363636\dots$ as a fraction in simplest form.
**Plan.** Let $x$ be the number and multiply by a power of ten that shifts one whole repeating block.
**Work.** $x = 0.363636\dots$, so $100x = 36.363636\dots$. Subtracting, $99x = 36$, hence $x = \frac{36}{99} = \frac{4}{11}$.
**Check.** $4 \div 11 = 0.363636\dots$, and the block 36 repeats.
@H3 | 7 · Multiplying decimals by counting places
**Question.** Work out $0.6 \times 0.45$.
**Plan.** Multiply as whole numbers, then restore the total number of decimal places.
**Work.** $6 \times 45 = 270$. There is one decimal place in 0.6 and two in 0.45, so three in total: 0.270.
**Answer.** 0.27.
**Check.** $0.6 \times 0.45$ is a little more than half of 0.45, so about 0.25; 0.27 is sensible.
@H3 | 8 · Percentage change in two steps
**Question.** A price of 240 is reduced by 15%, then the new price is reduced by 10%. Find the single equivalent reduction.
**Work.** Multipliers: $0.85 \times 0.90 = 0.765$. The final price is 76.5% of the original, so the reduction is 23.5%.
**Check.** A single 25% reduction would leave 180; two successive reductions leave $240 \times 0.765 = 183.6$, which is slightly more, as expected.
@H3 | 9 · Removing a tax that is already included
**Question.** A receipt shows 354 units including 18% VAT. Find the price before tax.
**Plan.** The total is 118% of the price, so divide by 1.18.
**Work.** $354 \div 1.18 = 300$. The VAT is 54 units.
**Check.** $300 \times 1.18 = 354$. Taking 18% of 354 would give 63.72, which is too large — a common error.
@H3 | 10 · Working backwards to an original amount
**Question.** After a 30% reduction a coat costs 84 units. Find the original price.
**Work.** The sale price is 70% of the original, so the original is $84 \div 0.7 = 120$.
**Check.** 30% of 120 is 36, and $120 - 36 = 84$.
@H2 | Algebra and equations
@H3 | 11 · Collecting like terms before solving
**Question.** Solve $5x + 3 - 2x = 18$.
**Work.** $3x + 3 = 18$, so $3x = 15$ and $x = 5$.
**Check.** $5(5) + 3 - 2(5) = 25 + 3 - 10 = 18$.
@H3 | 12 · Expanding then solving
**Question.** Solve $2(x - 3) = 14$.
**Plan.** Either divide both sides by 2 first, or expand. Dividing is quicker here.
**Work.** $x - 3 = 7$, so $x = 10$.
**Check.** $2(10 - 3) = 14$. Expanding first gives $2x - 6 = 14$ and the same answer, which is a useful second method.
@H3 | 13 · Forming an equation from a context
**Question.** A taxi charges 3 units plus 2 units per kilometre. A journey costs 27 units. How far was the journey?
**Plan.** Let $d$ be the distance in kilometres; the cost is $3 + 2d$.
**Work.** $3 + 2d = 27$, so $2d = 24$ and $d = 12$.
**Check.** $3 + 2(12) = 27$, and the answer is a distance in kilometres, not an amount of money.
@H3 | 14 · Substitution with a power
**Question.** Find the value of $3n^{2}$ when $n = -4$.
**Plan.** The power applies only to $n$, and squaring a negative gives a positive.
**Work.** $(-4)^{2} = 16$, and $3 \times 16 = 48$.
**Check.** $3n^{2}$ is not $(3n)^{2}$, which would be 144. The position of the bracket changes the answer.
@H2 | Shape and measurement
@H3 | 15 · Angles in a triangle with algebra
**Question.** The angles of a triangle are $x$, $2x + 10$ and $3x - 10$. Find each angle.
**Work.** $x + 2x + 10 + 3x - 10 = 180$, so $6x = 180$ and $x = 30$. The angles are 30°, 70° and 80°.
**Check.** They total 180° and all are less than 90°, so the triangle is acute-angled.
@H3 | 16 · Parallel lines and one known angle
**Question.** Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. One angle is 115°. Find the alternate angle and the co-interior angle.
**Work.** The alternate angle equals 115°; the co-interior angle is $180° - 115° = 65°$.
**Check.** Co-interior angles always total 180° between parallel lines, and 115 + 65 = 180.
@H3 | 17 · Interior angles of a regular polygon
**Question.** Find each interior angle of a regular 15-sided polygon.
**Work.** Interior sum $= (15 - 2) \times 180° = 2{,}340°$; each angle $= 2{,}340° \div 15 = 156°$.
**Check.** Each exterior angle is $360° \div 15 = 24°$, and $156 + 24 = 180$, as it must at every vertex.
@H3 | 18 · Area of a compound shape
**Question.** A rectangle 12 m by 8 m has a semicircle of diameter 8 m attached to one short side. Find the total area.
**Work.** Rectangle $= 96$ m²; semicircle radius 4 m $= \frac{1}{2}\pi(16) \approx 25.1$ m²; total $\approx 121.1$ m².
**Check.** The semicircle fits inside a 4 m by 8 m rectangle of area 32 m², and 25.1 is less than 32.
@H3 | 19 · Surface area of a cuboid
**Question.** Find the surface area of a cuboid 8 cm by 5 cm by 4 cm.
**Work.** $2(8 \times 5 + 8 \times 4 + 5 \times 4) = 2(40 + 32 + 20) = 184$ cm².
**Check.** The net has six rectangles in three equal pairs; 80 + 64 + 40 = 184.
@H3 | 20 · Capacity and volume units
**Question.** A tank is 1.2 m by 0.8 m by 0.5 m. Find its capacity in litres.
**Work.** Volume $= 0.48$ m³. Since 1 m³ = 1,000 litres, the capacity is 480 litres.
**Check.** $1.2 \times 0.8 = 0.96$ m² of base, and 0.96 × 0.5 = 0.48 m³; each cubic metre holds 1,000 litres.
@H2 | Sets and data
@H3 | 21 · A two-set problem with an overlap
**Question.** Of 40 students, 22 like football, 18 like basketball and 8 like both. How many like neither?
**Work.** $n(F \cup B) = 22 + 18 - 8 = 32$, so $40 - 32 = 8$ like neither.
**Check.** Only football is 14, only basketball is 10, both are 8: $14 + 10 + 8 = 32$, leaving 8.
@H3 | 22 · Mean, median and mode together
**Question.** Find the mean, median and mode of 4, 7, 7, 9, 13.
**Work.** Sum 40 over 5 values gives mean 8; the middle value is 7, so median 7; 7 occurs twice, so mode 7.
**Check.** The median equals the mode here but not in general; the mean is pulled up by the 13.
@H3 | 23 · Estimating a mean from grouped data
**Question.** Times are grouped as 10–14 (4), 15–19 (9), 20–24 (14). Estimate the mean.
**Work.** Midpoints 12, 17, 22: $48 + 153 + 308 = 509$ over 27 values, giving about 18.9.
**Check.** The estimate of 18.9 sits inside the class 15–19, which holds the most values, so it is plausible; a grouped estimate always treats each value as if it were at the midpoint.
@H3 | 24 · Reading a misleading graph
**Question.** A bar chart starts its vertical axis at 90. Two bars show 92 and 95. Explain the distortion.
**Work.** On a full scale from 0 the bars would differ by 3 in 100, about 3%; with the axis starting at 90 the visual difference is 3 in 5, or 60%.
**Check.** The data are unchanged; only the axis has changed, which is why the axis must be read before the picture.
@H3 | 25 · Choosing between area and volume
**Question.** A paint tin covers 12 m² per litre. A wall is 9 m by 3 m. How many litres are needed for two coats?
**Work.** Wall area $= 27$ m²; two coats cover 54 m²; $54 \div 12 = 4.5$ litres.
**Check.** One coat needs 2.25 litres, so two coats need 4.5 — and paint is sold in whole litres, so buy 5.

@LEAD | Marks are not awarded for length. The four responses below are short, and each one is annotated to show what earns credit under the MYP mathematics criteria and what holds a response back. Reading them is faster than reading a rubric, and more useful.
@H2 | Response 1 · Knowing and understanding (Criterion A)
@P | **The task.** “Explain why $\frac{3}{8}$ is a terminating decimal.”
@P | **The response.** “$8 = 2^{3}$, and $\frac{3}{8} = \frac{3 \times 125}{8 \times 125} = \frac{375}{1000} = 0.375$. The denominator is built only from 2s, so the fraction can always be written over a power of ten, and the decimal stops.”
@P | **Commentary.** Full credit. The student does more than state a fact: they name the condition, demonstrate it on the example, and give the reason the condition works. A response that says only “because the calculator shows 0.375” earns nothing, because a calculator result is not an explanation.
@H2 | Response 2 · Investigating patterns (Criterion B)
@P | **The task.** “How many matches are needed for stage $n$ of a pattern that starts with 4 matches and adds 3 each time?”
@P | **The response.** “Stage 1: 4. Stage 2: 7. Stage 3: 10. The differences are constant at 3, so the rule is $3n + 1$. Checking $n = 4$ gives 13, which matches the diagram. For stage 20, $3(20) + 1 = 61$.”
@P | **Commentary.** Full credit. Four things are present: data collected, a pattern described, a general rule expressed algebraically, and the rule verified against a case not used to find it. Without that verification the generalisation is a guess; with it, it is a justified conclusion.
@H2 | Response 3 · Communicating (Criterion C)
@P | **The task.** “Explain how to decide whether a problem needs area or volume.”
@P | **The response.** “Area is for something that covers — paint for a wall, turf for a garden, tiles for a floor. Volume is for something that fills — water in a tank, sand in a box. If the answer needs square units it is area; if it needs cubic units it is volume. A paint problem can still need volume at the end, because paint is sold by the litre, so the surface area is divided by the coverage.”
@P | **Commentary.** Full credit at the top band. The response defines the terms through examples, gives a decisive test, and then handles the awkward case where a real problem needs both. The last sentence is what lifts it above a correct but thin answer.
@H2 | Response 4 · Applying mathematics in context (Criterion D)
@P | **The task.** “A school needs to buy water for a sports day. Which option is better value?”
@P | **The response.** “Option A: 24 bottles of 500 ml for 6 units, which is 12 litres for 6 units, so 0.50 units per litre. Option B: one 5 litre container for 2.40 units, so 0.48 units per litre. Option B is cheaper per litre, but it needs cups and a way to keep it cold, and the bottles can be handed out and resealed. For 200 students needing about 0.5 litres each we need 100 litres: A costs 50 units, B costs 48 units. The difference is 2 units, which is smaller than the cost of cups, so A is the better decision.”
@P | **Commentary.** Full credit. The student computes both unit rates, models the real quantity needed, and then makes a justified decision that includes a factor the mathematics alone cannot supply. A response that stops at “0.48 is less than 0.50, so B” earns partial credit only, because it has not applied the result to the situation.

@SEC | Reference notes | Using a calculator well | Reference
@LEAD | A calculator performs the operations you enter, in the order you enter them. Most errors are errors of entry or of order, not of arithmetic.
@TABLE | Five habits that prevent calculator errors
Habit ; What it prevents
Estimate first ; Accepting an absurd answer
Use brackets for whole numerators and denominators ; $7+3 \div 2$ instead of $(7+3) \div 2$
Keep the full value in the calculator until the end ; Rounding errors accumulating
Write down intermediate values ; Losing the thread of a multi-step calculation
Check the units of the answer ; Reporting an area as a length
@P | A calculator is a tool for arithmetic, not for reasoning. It will compute $15\%$ of 240 correctly, but it will not decide whether 15% is the right percentage to use; it will divide by 0.7 when asked, but it will not know that dividing is the correct operation for finding an original price. The decisions remain yours, and they are what this course assesses.
@ERR | The three commonest calculator errors
Entering $3 + 4 \times 5$ as $(3 + 4) \times 5$; rounding a recurring decimal too early and then multiplying; and reading a display such as 1.2E3 as 1.23 rather than 1,200.
