@MOVE | A | Engage | A museum label, three ways of writing the same collection
@LEAD | The county museum has just finished counting its collection. The director needs one sentence for the new label, the cataloguer needs an exact record for the database, and the education officer needs a number a class can actually picture. The same collection produces three different numbers.
@TABLE | Figure 1.1 Three ways of describing one collection
Label on the gallery wall ; 12,000 objects
Catalogue entry ; 11,846 objects
Education leaflet ; nearly 12 thousand, of which about 4 hundred are on display
@P | Read the three records carefully. The wall label is short. The catalogue entry is exact. The leaflet adds a second idea: only part of the collection can be seen. All three are about the same museum, yet each answers a different question, and each has been written for a different reader.
@P | Now look at the numbers themselves. **11,846** contains five digits. Change the position of one digit and the value of the collection changes by thousands. Remove the **8** and the number becomes 1,146: a different museum. Remove the **1** from the front and the number becomes 1,846. Nothing about the shape of the digits has changed; only their positions have.
@P | The museum's education officer has a further problem. She wants a display board that reads **1,000 objects** beside **10,000 objects**, in the same size of digit.
@P | Set side by side, the two labels look almost identical: one extra zero, one extra comma. A visitor glancing at the board may not notice the difference, yet the second collection is ten times the size of the first. Somewhere in this chapter is the reason that **1,000** and **10,000** differ by a factor of ten even though they differ by a single character on the page.
@MINI | Notice and wonder
@LISTU
- Which of the three records would you put on the wall, and which would you put in the database? Why?
- The catalogue says 11,846. If the museum acquires 154 more objects, what would the wall label say?
- The leaflet says “about 4 hundred are on display”. Could the exact number be 384? Could it be 441?
- What does the zero in 11,846 contribute? What would happen to the number if that zero were removed?
@CALLOUT | Keep this question open until the end of the chapter: when a number is reported as “about 12,000”, has information been lost, or has the museum simply told us what we need?
@H2 | The three numbers behind every digit
@P | A written number carries three separate pieces of information, and good mathematical reading keeps them apart:
@LISTO
1. **The digit itself.** How many of that size of unit are there?
2. **The position of the digit.** Which size of unit is being counted — ones, tens, tenths?
3. **The precision of the report.** Has the number been rounded, and to what place?
@P | Most early mistakes in this chapter come from confusing the first two. The digit 4 is worth four, forty, four hundred or four tenths depending entirely on where it stands. A useful habit is to read a number aloud in place-value language before calculating with it: 11,846 is “eleven thousand, eight hundred and forty-six”, which is one ten-thousand, one thousand, eight hundreds, four tens and six ones.
@P | The third piece of information is the one adults use most and explain least. When the museum writes “about 12,000”, it is telling us that the exact count is not the point: the label is meant to convey scale. When a nurse records a temperature as 38.4 °C rather than 38.37 °C, the choice of precision is a decision about how much trust the instrument deserves. This chapter ends by asking you to make that decision deliberately.

@SEC | 1.1 | Number systems and the number line | Criterion A · i
@LEAD | Mathematicians use different number sets for different purposes. Distinguishing them is not a vocabulary game: the classification tells you which operations are always possible and which values count as solutions.
@DEF | Definitions: the number families
The **natural numbers** are the counting numbers $1, 2, 3, 4, …$
The **whole numbers** are the natural numbers together with zero: $0, 1, 2, 3, …$
The **integers** are the whole numbers and their negatives: $…, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …$
A **rational number** can be written as a fraction $\\frac{a}{b}$, where $a$ and $b$ are integers and $b \\ne 0$.
Every integer is also rational, because it can be written with denominator 1: $7 = \\frac{7}{1}$ and $-3 = \\frac{-3}{1}$. Terminating decimals are rational too: $0.25 = \\frac{25}{100}$. The families are nested — natural numbers sit inside the whole numbers, which sit inside the integers, which sit inside the rational numbers.
@VOCAB | Vocabulary you will use
natural number · whole number · integer · rational number · negative · positive · number line · order · inequality · precision · estimate
@FIG | c01_numline
@P | A number line is more than a display device. It gives a shared order to every integer, fraction and decimal. To compare two values, locate both: the one further to the right is greater. This stays true for negative numbers, where a larger digit means a smaller value.
@FIG | c01_order
@KEY | Symbols describe position, not magnitude alone
“$-5 < -2$” is correct because $-5$ lies to the left of $-2$, even though 5 is greater than 2. The symbol always points towards the smaller value, and the smaller value is the one further left. Reversing the symbol reverses the claim: $-5 > -2$ is false.
@H2 | Reading a negative number line
@P | Negative numbers are the first place where a number's written size and its position disagree. The digits in $-8$ are larger than the digits in $-3$, yet $-8$ is the smaller number. Two habits prevent the error:
@LISTU
- Say “eight below zero” rather than “minus eight”, so the direction is audible.
- Mark both numbers on a line before comparing. The further-left number is always the smaller one.
@P | The same logic orders temperatures, floors of a building, bank balances and positions below sea level. In each case zero is a boundary rather than an absence: 0 °C is a temperature, floor 0 is a floor, and 0 metres above sea level is a height.

@EX | 1.1.1 | Classifying with evidence
GIVEN: The numbers $0$, $7$, $-3$, $2.5$ and $\\frac{3}{4}$.
ASKED: State whether each number is natural, whole, an integer or rational. A number may belong to more than one family.
METHOD: Test the families in order from smallest to largest. A number that belongs to a larger family is written with evidence, not just a label.
STEPS:
1. $0$ is not natural, because counting begins at 1. It is whole, an integer and rational: $0 = \\frac{0}{1}$.
2. $7$ is natural, whole, an integer and rational: $7 = \\frac{7}{1}$.
3. $-3$ is an integer and rational $\\left(-3 = \\frac{-3}{1}\\right)$. It is not natural or whole because it is negative.
4. $2.5$ is rational because $2.5 = \\frac{5}{2}$. It is not an integer: the point lies between 2 and 3.
5. $\\frac{3}{4}$ is rational because both 3 and 4 are integers and the denominator is not zero.
ANSWER: $0$: whole, integer, rational. $7$: natural, whole, integer, rational. $-3$: integer, rational. $2.5$ and $\\frac{3}{4}$: rational.
CHECK: Every number in the list belongs to at least one family, and the largest family, rational, contains all of them.

@EX | 1.1.2 | A context with negative values
GIVEN: Overnight temperatures of $-6$ °C, $-3$ °C, $-1$ °C, $0$ °C and $4$ °C.
ASKED: Order the temperatures from coldest to warmest.
METHOD: Coldest means the least number, which is the furthest left on a number line.
STEPS:
1. Compare the negatives first: $-6 < -3 < -1$.
2. Zero is warmer than every negative value.
3. $4$ is warmer than zero.
ANSWER: $-6$ °C, $-3$ °C, $-1$ °C, $0$ °C, $4$ °C.
CHECK: Read the list back against the line: each value lies to the right of the one before it.
WATCH: Reading the absolute size of the numerals gives “$-8$ is bigger than $-3$ because 8 is bigger than 3”. Mark both values on a number line: the further-left number is smaller, so $-8 < -3$.

@EX | 1.1.3 | Placing fractions and decimals on the line
GIVEN: The values $\\frac{1}{2}$, $0.45$ and $\\frac{3}{5}$.
ASKED: Place them in order from least to greatest, and explain how the number line supports the order.
METHOD: Convert to a common form. Decimals to hundredths make the comparison immediate.
STEPS:
1. $\\frac{1}{2} = 0.5$ and $\\frac{3}{5} = 0.6$.
2. Compare in hundredths: $0.45 < 0.50 < 0.60$.
3. Check the positions on a number line between 0 and 1: 0.45 lies just left of the midpoint, 0.5 at the midpoint, 0.6 to its right.
ANSWER: $0.45 < \\frac{1}{2} < \\frac{3}{5}$.
CHECK: The differences are $0.05$ and $0.10$, so the second gap is twice the first. The line shows that too.

@SEC | 1.2 | Place value and the role of zero | Criterion A · i, ii
@LEAD | Our number system is called base ten because each position is worth ten times the position to its right. The digit 4 can stand for 4 ones, 4 tens, 4 hundreds or 4 tenths; the position supplies the value.
@FIG | c01_places
@DEF | Place value
A **place value** is the value of a digit determined by its position. In $3{,}048.307$, the digit 4 is in the tens place, so its value is $4 \\times 10 = 40$. The digit 7 is in the thousandths place, so its value is $7 \\times 0.001 = 0.007$.
@P | Moving one place to the left multiplies the value of a digit by 10. Moving one place to the right divides it by 10. This is why multiplying a number by 10 does not require inventing new digits: every digit shifts one place left. A zero appears at the end of a whole number because there are no units in that position, not because zero is being “added”.
@KEY | Zero has two jobs
As a **number**, zero marks the position on the number line between the positive and negative integers. As a **digit**, zero holds an empty place so that the other digits keep their intended values. Without the zero in 3,048, the digits would read 348 and mean a completely different quantity. Without the zero in 3.05, the digit 5 would shift into the tenths place and produce 3.5 — ten times too large.
@FIG | c01_dec
@H2 | Shifting the digits
@P | Multiply by 10 and every digit moves one place left; divide by 10 and every digit moves one place right. The decimal point does not travel as a trick. It marks the boundary between whole-number places and fractional places, and that boundary stays where it is while the digits move across it.
@TABLE | Multiplying and dividing by powers of ten
Original number ; Multiply by 10 ; Multiply by 100 ; Divide by 10
4.06 ; 40.6 ; 406 ; 0.406
37.5 ; 375 ; 3,750 ; 3.75
0.8 ; 8 ; 80 ; 0.08
@ERR | Adding a zero is not the same as multiplying by ten
Adding a zero to the whole number 4 gives 40, which is ten times as large. Adding a zero after the decimal part of 4.5 gives 4.50, which is the same number. The rule depends on **where** the zero is inserted, not merely that one more zero appears. Insert a zero inside a number and the other digits change place: 4.05 is not 4.5.

@EX | 1.2.1 | Reading place value
GIVEN: The number $6{,}502.94$.
ASKED: State the value of each underlined digit: (a) the 5, (b) the 4, (c) the 9.
METHOD: Name the place first, then multiply the digit by the value of that place. Reading the position before the digit prevents most errors.
STEPS:
1. The 5 is in the hundreds place. Its value is $5 \\times 100 = 500$.
2. The 4 is the second digit after the decimal point, in the hundredths place. Its value is $4 \\times 0.01 = 0.04$.
3. The 9 is the first digit after the decimal point, in the tenths place. Its value is $9 \\times 0.1 = 0.9$.
ANSWER: (a) 500 (b) 0.04 (c) 0.9
CHECK: The three values are very different even though the digits are all single digits. Position, not digit, decides value.

@EX | 1.2.2 | Shifting by powers of ten
GIVEN: (a) $4.06 \\times 100$ (b) $37.5 \\div 10$ (c) $0.8 \\times 1{,}000$.
ASKED: Calculate each value and explain the movement of the digits.
METHOD: Each multiplication by 10 moves every digit one place left; each division by 10 moves every digit one place right. For 100, move two places.
STEPS:
1. $4.06 \\times 100 = 406$: every digit moves two places left, so the tenths digit 0 becomes a unit and the hundredths digit 6 becomes a ten.
2. $37.5 \\div 10 = 3.75$: every digit moves one place right.
3. $0.8 \\times 1{,}000 = 800$: eight tenths of a thousand is eight hundred.
ANSWER: (a) 406 (b) 3.75 (c) 800
CHECK: Estimate first. $4 \\times 100 = 400$, and 406 is close. $37 \\div 10$ is about 3.7. Eight tenths of a thousand is 800, not 8,000.

@EX | 1.2.3 | Which is larger: 3.5 or 3.05?
GIVEN: Two decimals that look similar when written quickly.
ASKED: Decide which is larger, using place value rather than the number of digits.
METHOD: Compare digit by digit from the left, in the same place. Never compare the total number of digits.
STEPS:
1. Both numbers have 3 units, so compare the tenths: 3.5 has 5 tenths, 3.05 has 0 tenths.
2. Since $5 > 0$, the comparison is decided in the tenths place.
3. Write 3.05 as 3.050 if you need the same number of decimal places: $3.500 > 3.050$.
ANSWER: $3.5 > 3.05$. The extra digit in 3.05 does not make it larger.
CHECK: On a number line, 3.05 lies just past 3 and 3.5 lies halfway to 4.
WATCH: “3.05 has more digits, so it is bigger.” Digits after the decimal point do not add size unless they are in a place that carries value.

@SEC | 1.3 | Expanded form and equivalent representations | Criterion C · i, ii
@LEAD | Expanded form takes a number apart so that every digit can be seen with the value it contributes. Written both ways, a number becomes something you can check, compare and reconstruct.
@DEF | Expanded form
**Expanded form** writes a number as the sum of the values of its digits. For whole numbers each term is a digit multiplied by a power of ten:
$3{,}048 = 3 \\times 1{,}000 + 0 \\times 100 + 4 \\times 10 + 8 \\times 1$
For decimals the same idea continues to the right of the decimal point:
$40.607 = 4 \\times 10 + 0 \\times 1 + 6 \\times 0.1 + 0 \\times 0.01 + 7 \\times 0.001$
The zero terms may be left out, but writing them makes the structure visible: every place is accounted for, including the empty ones.
@VOCAB | Vocabulary you will use
place value · digit · expanded form · standard form · equivalent · tenths · hundredths · thousandths · round · word form
@H2 | Why expanded form is worth the effort
@P | Expanded form is the written record of the place-value system. Three things become possible once a number is written this way.
@LISTU
- **Checking.** If a digit has been written in the wrong place, the expanded sum will not match the number you meant.
- **Reconstructing.** Given the expanded sum, you can rebuild the number without guessing where each digit belongs.
- **Comparing.** Two numbers in expanded form can be compared term by term, starting from the largest place.
@P | In later chapters expanded form becomes the bridge to index notation: $3 \\times 1{,}000$ is $3 \\times 10^{3}$. The expanded sum you write now is the same idea that will later be compressed into powers.
@KEY | Equivalent representations of the same quantity
$0.7$, $0.70$ and $\\frac{7}{10}$ all name the same number. So do $2\\frac{1}{2}$, 2.5 and $\\frac{5}{2}$. Writing zeros after the last decimal digit does not change the value, because those zeros contribute $0 \\times 0.01$ and so on. This is exactly why expanded form can be used as a check: the zero terms add nothing.
@ERR | Zero terms are not optional in the reasoning
Leaving out a zero term when you write the number is fine; forgetting that the place exists is not. If 3,048 is expanded as $3 \\times 1{,}000 + 4 \\times 10 + 8$, the reader cannot tell whether the hundreds place is zero or missing. Writing $0 \\times 100$ removes the doubt.

@EX | 1.3.1 | Writing a whole number in expanded form and in words
GIVEN: The number $5{,}207$.
ASKED: (a) Write it in expanded form using all four places. (b) Write it in words. (c) Explain which digit is doing the least work.
METHOD: Read the places from left to right: thousands, hundreds, tens, ones. Write each digit multiplied by its place value.
STEPS:
1. Thousands: $5 \times 1{,}000$. Hundreds: $2 \times 100$. Tens: $0 \times 10$. Ones: $7 \times 1$.
2. Join the terms: $5{,}207 = 5 \times 1{,}000 + 2 \times 100 + 0 \times 10 + 7 \times 1$.
3. In words: five thousand, two hundred and seven.
ANSWER: (a) $5 \times 1{,}000 + 2 \times 100 + 0 \times 10 + 7 \times 1$ (b) five thousand, two hundred and seven (c) The zero contributes nothing to the total, but it holds the tens place so that the 2 keeps the value 200 rather than 20.
CHECK: Add the terms: $5{,}000 + 200 + 0 + 7 = 5{,}207$, which matches the original number.
WATCH: Reading the digits without their places can produce “five thousand and twenty-seven”, which would be 5,027. Say each place aloud as you expand.

@EX | 1.3.2 | Rebuilding a number from an expanded sum
GIVEN: $6 \\times 100 + 0 \\times 10 + 3 \\times 1 + 9 \\times 0.01 + 4 \\times 0.001$.
ASKED: Write the number in standard form and state the value of the 9.
METHOD: Allocate each term to its column, then read the columns from left to right.
STEPS:
1. $6 \\times 100 = 600$ goes in the hundreds column.
2. $0 \\times 10$ means the tens column is empty, so write 0.
3. $3 \\times 1 = 3$ goes in the ones column; the decimal point follows.
4. $9 \\times 0.01$ goes in the hundredths column, so the tenths column needs a 0.
5. $4 \\times 0.001$ goes in the thousandths column.
ANSWER: $603.094$. The 9 has value 0.09.
CHECK: Read the number back: 603.094 has 6 hundreds, 0 tens, 3 ones, 0 tenths, 9 hundredths and 4 thousandths. Every term is accounted for.
WATCH: Writing 603.94 loses a place. The 9 was hundredths, not tenths, so a zero must be written between the decimal point and the 9.

@EX | 1.3.3 | The same quantity, three ways
GIVEN: The quantity two and a half litres.
ASKED: Write it as a mixed number, as a decimal and in expanded form; explain what changes and what does not.
METHOD: Choose the form that makes the next operation easiest, then check that the value is unchanged.
STEPS:
1. As a mixed number: $2\\frac{1}{2}$ litres.
2. As a decimal: 2.5 litres, because $\\frac{1}{2} = 0.5$.
3. In expanded form: $2 \\times 1 + 5 \\times 0.1$ litres, which is $2 + 0.5$.
ANSWER: $2\\frac{1}{2}$ L = 2.5 L = $2 \\times 1 + 5 \\times 0.1$ L. The representation changes; the quantity does not.
CHECK: Pour 2.5 litres into a 2.5-litre bottle: it fills exactly. The form of the number has no effect on the amount of water.

@SEC | 1.4 | Comparing, ordering and rounding | Criterion A · i, ii, iii
@LEAD | Comparing is a place-value skill, not a digit-counting skill. Rounding is a decision about precision: it replaces a number with the nearest value at a chosen place. Both depend on the same system of positions.
@H2 | Comparing decimals
@P | Compare from the left, one place at a time, and stop as soon as the digits differ. Never compare the number of digits after the decimal point: 0.8 has fewer digits than 0.75 but is larger.
@LISTO
1. Write both numbers with the same number of decimal places, using trailing zeros.
2. Align the decimal points and compare the digits in each column from left to right.
3. The first column where the digits differ decides the comparison.
@METHOD | A reliable comparison routine
Write 0.8 and 0.75 with the same number of places: 0.80 and 0.75. Compare the tenths: 8 tenths against 7 tenths. Since $8 > 7$, we have $0.80 > 0.75$, so $0.8 > 0.75$. The comparison needed only one column.
@H2 | Ordering a mixed list
@P | When a list mixes positive and negative numbers, split it first. Every negative number is less than every positive number, and zero sits between them. Order the negatives by remembering that a larger digit after the minus sign means a smaller value.
@H2 | Rounding
@DEF | Rounding to a place
To **round** a number to a given place, look at the digit immediately to the right of that place. If it is 5 or more, increase the digit in the place by 1; if it is 4 or less, leave it unchanged. Then replace every digit to the right with zeros (before the decimal point) or remove them (after the decimal point).
@FIG | c01_round
@P | Rounding is not “making the number bigger” or “making it smaller”. It is choosing the nearest value at the required precision. 3,486 rounded to the nearest hundred is 3,500 because 3,486 is 14 away from 3,500 and 86 away from 3,400. Rounded to the nearest thousand, the same number is 3,000.
@KEY | Every rounded answer should be readable as a decision
When you round, say the place aloud: “to the nearest hundred”, “to two decimal places”. A rounded number without its place is a claim without evidence, because 3,500 could be the rounding of anything from 3,450 to 3,549.
@ERR | Two rounding mistakes to avoid
**Mistake 1: rounding twice.** Rounding 4.47 to one decimal place by first rounding to 4.5 and then to 5 gives the wrong answer; the correct answer is 4.5, because the digit after the tenths is 7.
**Mistake 2: dropping the zeros.** 2,958 rounded to the nearest hundred is 3,000, not 30. The zeros record which places are now unknown.

@EX | 1.4.1 | Ordering a mixed list
GIVEN: $-2.5$, $0.4$, $-2.05$, $3$, $-0.5$.
ASKED: Order the numbers from least to greatest.
METHOD: Separate the negatives, order them using place value, then attach zero and the positive numbers.
STEPS:
1. Negatives: $-2.5$, $-2.05$ and $-0.5$. Compare the sizes 2.5, 2.05 and 0.5: $2.5 > 2.05 > 0.5$.
2. For negatives the order reverses: $-2.5 < -2.05 < -0.5$.
3. The positive values are 0.4 and 3, with $0.4 < 3$.
ANSWER: $-2.5 < -2.05 < -0.5 < 0.4 < 3$.
CHECK: Place all five on a number line. Each value should lie to the right of the one before it.
WATCH: “$-2.05$ is bigger than $-2.5$ because 205 is bigger than 25.” It is bigger in magnitude but further left on the line, so it is smaller: $-2.05 > -2.5$ is correct only as a magnitude statement, and wrong as a value statement.

@EX | 1.4.2 | Rounding a whole number to different places
GIVEN: The number $3{,}846$.
ASKED: Round it to (a) the nearest ten (b) the nearest hundred (c) the nearest thousand.
METHOD: Identify the place, then look at the single digit to its right.
STEPS:
1. Nearest ten: look at the ones digit 6, which is 5 or more, so 3,846 becomes 3,850.
2. Nearest hundred: look at the tens digit 4, which is less than 5, so 3,846 becomes 3,800.
3. Nearest thousand: look at the hundreds digit 8, which is 5 or more, so 3,846 becomes 4,000.
ANSWER: (a) 3,850 (b) 3,800 (c) 4,000
CHECK: Each answer lies within half a unit of the rounding place from 3,846: 4 from 3,850, 46 from 3,800 and 154 from 4,000.

@EX | 1.4.3 | Rounding decimals
GIVEN: The number $0.4736$.
ASKED: Round it to (a) two decimal places (b) one decimal place (c) the nearest whole number.
METHOD: Count decimal places from the point, then use the single digit immediately to the right of the last place you keep.
STEPS:
1. Two decimal places: keep 0.47, look at 3, which is less than 5, so the answer is 0.47.
2. One decimal place: keep 0.4, look at 7, which is 5 or more, so the answer is 0.5.
3. Nearest whole number: look at the tenths digit 4, which is less than 5, so the answer is 0.
ANSWER: (a) 0.47 (b) 0.5 (c) 0
CHECK: Each answer is the nearest value at that precision. 0.4736 is closer to 0.47 than 0.48, closer to 0.5 than 0.4, and closer to 0 than to 1.

@EX | 1.4.4 | Choosing a sensible accuracy
GIVEN: A school records 1,247 students; a shop sells an item for 12.48; a nurse records a temperature of 37.6 °C.
ASKED: State a sensible precision for each record and justify it.
METHOD: Ask what decision the number will support. Extra digits are only useful if they change the decision.
STEPS:
1. Student numbers are counted in whole people, so 1,247 is exact; a newsletter may round it to “about 1,250” for readability.
2. Money is recorded to the nearest cent because that is the smallest unit in use: 12.48, not 12.481.
3. A clinical thermometer reads to one decimal place, so 37.6 °C records the instrument's precision; writing 37.63 would claim accuracy the instrument does not have.
ANSWER: 1,247 (or 1,250 in a newsletter) · 12.48 · 37.6 °C. In each case the precision matches the unit that matters.
CHECK: Would an extra digit change any decision? If not, the extra digit is decoration rather than information.

@SEC | 1.5 | Estimation and sensible accuracy | Criterion D · i, ii, iii
@LEAD | An estimate is a calculation you can do in your head with numbers you can hold. Its purpose is not to replace the exact answer but to tell you whether the exact answer is plausible.
@DEF | Estimating by rounding
To **estimate** a calculation, round each number to a convenient place, carry out the simpler calculation, and state the rounding you used. An estimate should be labelled: “about 8,000, using numbers rounded to the nearest hundred”.
@H2 | Two habits that make estimates useful
@LISTU
- **Round in one direction on purpose.** If you round every number up, the estimate is certainly too high; if you round every number down, it is certainly too low. That knowledge is often more valuable than the estimate itself.
- **Compare, then decide.** An exact answer far from your estimate suggests a keying error, a misplaced decimal point or a wrong operation. An exact answer close to the estimate is evidence that the calculation is sound.
@KEY | Front-end estimation
For a sum, add the leading digits first and then adjust: $4{,}876 + 3{,}142$ is about $4{,}000 + 3{,}000 = 7{,}000$, and the remaining parts add roughly $900 + 100 = 1{,}000$, so the total is about 8,000. Front-end estimation keeps the size of the answer in view while you work.

@EX | 1.5.1 | Estimating a sum and judging the estimate
GIVEN: $4{,}876 + 3{,}142$.
ASKED: Estimate the sum, then calculate it exactly and compare.
METHOD: Round each number to the nearest hundred, add, then compare with the exact value.
STEPS:
1. $4{,}876 \\approx 4{,}900$ and $3{,}142 \\approx 3{,}100$.
2. Estimate: $4{,}900 + 3{,}100 = 8{,}000$.
3. Exact: $4{,}876 + 3{,}142 = 8{,}018$.
ANSWER: The estimate is 8,000; the exact sum is 8,018, which is 18 more. The estimate is reasonable: it is within 1% of the exact value.
CHECK: Both numbers were rounded in opposite directions — one up by 24, one down by 42 — so the estimate should be slightly low, and it is.

@EX | 1.5.2 | Estimating a product in a real context
GIVEN: A class orders 38 workbooks at 21 units each.
ASKED: Estimate the total cost and say whether the estimate is more likely to be too high or too low.
METHOD: Round both factors to convenient numbers whose product is easy, then reason about the direction of the rounding.
STEPS:
1. $38 \\approx 40$ (rounded up) and $21 \\approx 20$ (rounded down).
2. Estimate: $40 \\times 20 = 800$ units.
3. Exact: $38 \\times 21 = 798$ units.
ANSWER: About 800 units; the exact cost is 798, so the estimate is 2 units too high. Because one factor was rounded up by 2 and the other down by 1, the two errors nearly cancel.
CHECK: $38 \\times 21 = 38 \\times 20 + 38 = 760 + 38 = 798$.

@EX | 1.5.3 | Choosing the accuracy the decision needs
GIVEN: A community centre must buy paint for a wall of area 47.6 m². One litre covers 12 m² and paint is sold only in whole litres.
ASKED: How many litres should be bought, and why is the rounded calculation not simply “round the answer”?
METHOD: Calculate, then round in the direction that keeps the job possible.
STEPS:
1. $47.6 \\div 12 \\approx 3.97$ litres.
2. Rounding to the nearest whole litre gives 4 litres — but 4 litres cover $48$ m², which is just enough.
3. Because paint cannot be bought in part-litres and running short would stop the job, round **up** to the next whole litre: 4 litres.
ANSWER: 4 litres. Rounding up is a decision about risk, not about the nearest number.
CHECK: $4 \\times 12 = 48$ m², which is greater than 47.6 m². Three litres would cover only 36 m².

@H2 | What this chapter established
@LISTU
- Numbers belong to nested families: natural numbers inside whole numbers, inside integers, inside rational numbers.
