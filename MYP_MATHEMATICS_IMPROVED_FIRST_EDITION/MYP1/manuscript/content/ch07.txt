@MOVE | A | Engage | Three lines, one impossible triangle
@LEAD | A student cuts three strips of card: 4 cm, 5 cm and 12 cm, and tries to make a triangle. The strips will not close: however the two shorter strips are arranged, they cannot reach across the longest one.
@P | The reason is arithmetic. The two shorter sides together (4 + 5 = 9 cm) are shorter than the longest side (12 cm), so the triangle cannot exist. Geometry is not only drawing: it makes statements that either can or cannot be true, and the check is often a calculation.
@P | The same is true of angles. The three angles of any triangle add to exactly 180°, whatever the triangle looks like. That is not a coincidence of drawing but a consequence of how straight lines behave — and this chapter builds the argument.

@SEC | 7.1 | Points, lines, angles and notation | Criterion A · i, ii
@LEAD | Geometry has its own vocabulary and notation, and precision in naming is part of the mathematics. A wrong label can make a correct calculation unreadable.
@DEF | Naming and measuring
A **point** marks a position and is named with a capital letter. A **line** extends without end in both directions; a **line segment** has two endpoints; a **ray** starts at a point and extends in one direction.
An **angle** is formed where two rays meet at a **vertex**. It is measured in **degrees** (°) and named with three letters, with the vertex in the middle: angle $ABC$ has its vertex at $B$.
@FIG | c07_types
@TABLE | Angle types
Type ; Size ; Example in a clock face
Acute ; between 0° and 90° ; 2 o'clock (60°)
Right ; exactly 90° ; 3 o'clock
Obtuse ; between 90° and 180° ; 5 o'clock (150°)
Straight ; exactly 180° ; 6 o'clock
Reflex ; between 180° and 360° ; 8 o'clock (240°)
@KEY | Notation that is worth learning exactly
@LISTU
- $AB$ means the length of the segment from $A$ to $B$; the line through them is written $\\overleftrightarrow{AB}$.
- $\\angle ABC$ names the angle at $B$ between $BA$ and $BC$.
- Two lines meeting at right angles are **perpendicular**; lines that never meet however far they extend are **parallel**.
- Angles on the same arc are marked with matching arcs; equal angles are shown with identical arc marks.
@METHOD | Measuring and drawing an angle
@LISTO
1. Place the centre of the protractor on the vertex, with the baseline along one arm.
2. Read the scale that starts at 0° on that arm — there are two scales, and using the wrong one gives an answer that is wrong by the supplement.
3. To draw an angle, draw the first arm, place the centre at the vertex, mark the required number of degrees, then join.
4. Check the result is sensible: an acute angle must look smaller than a right angle.
@ERR | Reading the wrong scale
A protractor has two scales, inner and outer. Reading the outer scale when the arm lies on the inner zero gives $180° - \\theta$ instead of $\\theta$. If the measured angle is 150° when the drawing is clearly acute, the wrong scale has been read.

@EX | 7.1.1 | Naming and classifying
GIVEN: Three rays from a point $O$: $OA$ at 0°, $OB$ at 62°, $OC$ at 145°.
ASKED: Name and classify angle $AOB$, angle $BOC$ and angle $AOC$.
METHOD: Subtract the readings and classify by size.
STEPS:
1. $\\angle AOB = 62° - 0° = 62°$, acute.
2. $\\angle BOC = 145° - 62° = 83°$, acute.
3. $\\angle AOC = 145° - 0° = 145°$, obtuse.
ANSWER: 62° acute, 83° acute, 145° obtuse.
CHECK: $\\angle AOB + \\angle BOC = \\angle AOC$, as the rays lie in order.

@EX | 7.1.2 | Using a right angle
GIVEN: Two straight lines crossing, with one angle marked 37°.
ASKED: Find the three other angles at the crossing.
METHOD: Use the straight-line property and the equality of vertically opposite angles.
STEPS:
1. The angle next to 37° on a straight line is $180° - 37° = 143°$.
2. Vertically opposite angles are equal, so the other two are 37° and 143°.
ANSWER: 37°, 143°, 143°.
CHECK: $37 + 143 + 37 + 143 = 360°$, a full turn.

@EX | 7.1.3 | A reasoning step with notation
GIVEN: $A$, $B$ and $C$ lie on a straight line in that order, with $\\angle ABD = 118°$.
ASKED: Find $\\angle DBC$, giving a reason.
METHOD: Angles on a straight line sum to 180°.
STEPS:
1. $\\angle ABD + \\angle DBC = 180°$ (angles on a straight line).
2. $\\angle DBC = 180° - 118° = 62°$.
ANSWER: 62°.
CHECK: $118 + 62 = 180$.
WATCH: Naming the reason is part of the answer. A figure without a reason earns fewer marks than a figure with one.

@SEC | 7.2 | Angle relationships | Criterion A · ii; Criterion C · iii
@LEAD | Three relationships do most of the work in angle problems: angles on a straight line, angles at a point, and vertically opposite angles. Each is a consequence of a full turn being 360°.
@FIG | c07_angles
@DEF | Three angle facts
@LISTU
- **Angles on a straight line** add to 180°.
- **Angles at a point** add to 360°.
- **Vertically opposite angles** — the angles opposite each other where two lines cross — are equal.
@KEY | Why all three follow from one idea
A full turn is 360°. Two angles that together make a straight line make half a turn, so they add to 180°. Angles around a point make a whole turn, so they add to 360°. Vertically opposite angles are equal because each is $180°$ minus the same adjacent angle.
@METHOD | Solving an angle problem with reasons
@LISTO
1. Mark on the diagram what you know and what you want.
2. Choose the relationship that connects them: straight line, point, or vertically opposite.
3. Write an equation; solve it.
4. State the reason with the answer, in words or in brackets.
5. Check that the angles are plausible: no angle in a triangle or on a line can be negative.
@TABLE | Which relationship to use
Diagram feature ; Relationship ; Equation
Two angles making a straight line ; sum to 180° ; $a + b = 180$
Angles all the way round a point ; sum to 360° ; $a + b + c + d = 360$
Two crossing lines ; vertically opposite equal ; $a = c$
Three angles on a line ; sum to 180° ; $a + b + c = 180$
@ERR | Assuming a diagram is to scale
A diagram may show an angle that looks like 90° but is labelled 85°. Never measure a diagram to find an answer, and never assume two lines are parallel because they look parallel. Use the given information and the named relationships only.

@EX | 7.2.1 | Angles on a straight line
GIVEN: Three angles on a straight line are $x$, $2x$ and $3x + 30$.
ASKED: Find $x$ and the three angles.
METHOD: Use the straight-line sum and solve the equation.
STEPS:
1. $x + 2x + 3x + 30 = 180$.
2. $6x = 150$.
3. $x = 25$, so the angles are 25°, 50° and 105°.
ANSWER: $x = 25$; angles 25°, 50°, 105°.
CHECK: $25 + 50 + 105 = 180$.

@EX | 7.2.2 | Vertically opposite angles
GIVEN: Two straight lines cross. One angle is $4a - 10$ and the vertically opposite angle is $2a + 30$.
ASKED: Find $a$ and the size of both angles.
METHOD: Set the vertically opposite angles equal.
STEPS:
1. $4a - 10 = 2a + 30$.
2. $2a = 40$, so $a = 20$.
3. Each angle is $4(20) - 10 = 70°$.
ANSWER: $a = 20$; both angles 70°.
CHECK: $2(20) + 30 = 70$; the adjacent angles are 110° each, and $70 + 110 + 70 + 110 = 360$.

@EX | 7.2.3 | Angles at a point
GIVEN: Five angles meet at a point. Four are 40°, 65°, 90° and 105°.
ASKED: Find the fifth, with a reason.
METHOD: Use the point sum.
STEPS:
1. $40 + 65 + 90 + 105 = 300$.
2. $360 - 300 = 60$.
ANSWER: 60°, because angles at a point sum to 360°.
CHECK: $300 + 60 = 360$.
WATCH: A common slip is to use 180° at a point. Check whether the angles surround the point completely or lie on one side of a line.

@SEC | 7.3 | Parallel lines and transversals | Criterion A · ii, iii
@LEAD | When a line crosses two parallel lines, eight angles appear, and they fall into only two sizes. That is a strong result, and it is the basis of all reasoning about parallel lines.
@FIG | c07_parallel
@DEF | Corresponding and alternate angles
A **transversal** is a line that crosses two or more other lines. When it crosses **parallel** lines:
@LISTU
- **Corresponding angles** — in the same position at each crossing — are equal.
- **Alternate angles** — on opposite sides of the transversal, between the parallel lines — are equal.
- **Co-interior angles** — on the same side of the transversal, between the parallel lines — add to 180°.
@KEY | Why only two sizes appear
Corresponding angles are equal because a translation along the transversal maps one crossing onto the other. Once corresponding angles are equal, alternate angles follow by vertically opposite angles, and co-interior angles follow from a straight line. One fact generates the other two.
@METHOD | Finding angles between parallel lines
@LISTO
1. Identify the parallel lines and mark them with matching arrows.
2. Identify the transversal.
3. Find a corresponding or alternate partner for the angle you know.
4. Continue step by step, naming the relationship each time, until you reach the required angle.
@TABLE | The three relationships in one diagram
Relationship ; Position ; What is true
Corresponding ; same corner at each crossing ; equal
Alternate ; opposite sides, inside ; equal
Co-interior ; same side, inside ; sum to 180°
@ERR | Using alternate angles that are not alternate
Z-shaped pairs of angles are alternate and equal. C-shaped pairs inside the parallel lines are co-interior and supplementary, not equal. The letter shape is a useful check: Z means equal, C means add to 180°.

@EX | 7.3.1 | One angle, then the rest
GIVEN: A transversal crosses two parallel lines, and one angle is 68°.
ASKED: Find the alternate angle, the corresponding angle and the co-interior angle, with reasons.
METHOD: Apply each relationship in turn.
STEPS:
1. Alternate angle: 68°, alternate angles are equal.
2. Corresponding angle: 68°, corresponding angles are equal.
3. Co-interior angle: $180° - 68° = 112°$, co-interior angles sum to 180°.
ANSWER: 68°, 68°, 112°.
CHECK: All eight angles are 68° or 112°, and the sizes alternate around each crossing.

@EX | 7.3.2 | An algebraic parallel-line problem
GIVEN: Two parallel lines cut by a transversal. A corresponding pair is $3x + 15$ and $5x - 25$.
ASKED: Find $x$ and the angle.
METHOD: Set corresponding angles equal.
STEPS:
1. $3x + 15 = 5x - 25$.
2. $40 = 2x$, so $x = 20$.
3. Angle: $3(20) + 15 = 75°$.
ANSWER: $x = 20$; the angles are 75°.
CHECK: $5(20) - 25 = 75$.

@EX | 7.3.3 | Deciding whether lines are parallel
GIVEN: A transversal crosses two lines. A pair of alternate angles measures 74° and 76°.
ASKED: Decide whether the lines are parallel.
METHOD: Use the converse of the alternate-angle property.
STEPS:
1. If the lines were parallel, the alternate angles would be equal.
2. $74° \\neq 76°$, so the lines are not parallel.
ANSWER: Not parallel; the alternate angles differ by 2°.
CHECK: Two lines meeting at a very small angle look nearly parallel, which is why the calculation matters.
WATCH: “They look parallel” is not a reason. The converse properties are the test.

@SEC | 7.4 | Triangles | Criterion A · ii, iii; Criterion B · iii
@LEAD | A triangle is the simplest closed shape and the most constrained: three angles summing to 180° and three sides obeying the triangle inequality. Both facts generate a great deal of reasoning.
@FIG | c07_sum
@DEF | Triangles by sides and by angles
By sides: **equilateral** (three equal sides), **isosceles** (two equal sides), **scalene** (no equal sides). By angles: **acute**, **right-angled**, **obtuse**.
The angles of any triangle add to 180°. In an isosceles triangle the angles opposite the equal sides are equal.
@KEY | Why the angle sum is 180°
Draw a line through the apex parallel to the base. The two base angles then match alternate angles on that line, and the apex angle sits between them. Together the three angles make a straight line, so they sum to 180°. The proof uses only the parallel-line property, which is why that property was established first.
@TABLE | Angle facts for special triangles
Triangle ; Angles ; Reason
Equilateral ; 60°, 60°, 60° ; three equal angles summing to 180°
Right-angled ; one angle 90°, other two sum to 90° ; angle sum
Isosceles ; two equal base angles ; equal sides give equal angles
Obtuse ; one angle above 90° ; definition
@METHOD | Finding unknown angles in a triangle
@LISTO
1. Write the angle sum equation: the three angles total 180°.
2. Use any extra information: equal sides give equal angles, a right angle is 90°.
3. Solve for the unknown.
4. Check the answer against the type of triangle: an equilateral triangle must give 60° three times.
@ERR | Using 360° for a triangle
The 360° figure applies to angles at a point, not inside a triangle. A quick check: a triangle fits inside a straight angle, so its total must be less than 180° plus the exterior angle — and exactly 180°.

@EX | 7.4.1 | Two angles given
GIVEN: A triangle has angles 48° and 67°.
ASKED: Find the third angle and classify the triangle.
METHOD: Subtract from 180°.
STEPS:
1. $48 + 67 = 115$.
2. $180 - 115 = 65$.
ANSWER: 65°; all angles acute, so the triangle is acute-angled.
CHECK: $48 + 67 + 65 = 180$.

@EX | 7.4.2 | An isosceles triangle
GIVEN: An isosceles triangle has a vertex angle of 40°.
ASKED: Find the base angles, with reasons.
METHOD: Use the angle sum and the equality of base angles.
STEPS:
1. The base angles are equal, so each is $x$ with $2x + 40 = 180$.
2. $2x = 140$, so $x = 70$.
ANSWER: Each base angle is 70°, because the angles opposite the equal sides are equal.
CHECK: $40 + 70 + 70 = 180$.

@EX | 7.4.3 | A triangle with algebra
GIVEN: The angles of a triangle are $x$, $2x + 10$ and $3x - 10$.
ASKED: Find each angle and classify the triangle.
METHOD: Form the angle-sum equation.
STEPS:
1. $x + 2x + 10 + 3x - 10 = 180$.
2. $6x = 180$, so $x = 30$.
3. Angles: 30°, 70°, 80°.
ANSWER: 30°, 70°, 80°; acute-angled.
CHECK: $30 + 70 + 80 = 180$.
WATCH: A negative or zero angle is a signal that the equation is wrong, not that the triangle is unusual.

@SEC | 7.5 | Polygons and their angles | Criterion A · ii, iii; Criterion C · ii
@LEAD | A polygon is a closed flat shape made of straight sides that do not cross. The angles of any polygon can be found from the angles of triangles, and that is why the triangle result in 7.4 is worth so much.
@DEF | Polygons, regular and irregular
A **polygon** has straight sides and no curves. It is **regular** when every side is the same length and every interior angle is equal; otherwise it is **irregular**. A polygon is **convex** when every interior angle is less than 180°, so no corner points inwards.
@FIG | c07_polygon
@KEY | The interior angle sum
Drawing all the diagonals from one vertex splits an $n$-sided polygon into $n - 2$ triangles. Each triangle holds 180°, so the interior angles of the polygon total
$(n - 2) \times 180°$
For a quadrilateral: $2 \times 180° = 360°$. For a pentagon: $540°$. For a hexagon: $720°$. For a decagon: $1440°$.
@METHOD | Working with regular polygons
**One interior angle:** divide the total by $n$. A regular pentagon has $540° \div 5 = 108°$.
**Exterior angle:** each exterior angle is $360° \div n$, because the exterior angles of any convex polygon add to a full turn. A regular pentagon has $360° \div 5 = 72°$.
**Check:** interior + exterior $= 180°$ at every vertex, since they sit on a straight line.
@TABLE | Angle facts for regular polygons
Polygon ; Sides $n$ ; Interior sum ; Each interior ; Each exterior
Triangle ; 3 ; $180°$ ; $60°$ ; $120°$
Quadrilateral ; 4 ; $360°$ ; $90°$ ; $90°$
Pentagon ; 5 ; $540°$ ; $108°$ ; $72°$
Hexagon ; 6 ; $720°$ ; $120°$ ; $60°$
Octagon ; 8 ; $1080°$ ; $135°$ ; $45°$
Decagon ; 10 ; $1440°$ ; $144°$ ; $36°$
@ERR | Assuming every quadrilateral is a rectangle
A rectangle has four right angles, but a quadrilateral need not. In a kite or a trapezium the four angles still total 360°, yet none of them may be 90°. The sum is fixed; the individual angles are not.
@KEY | Why the exterior angles total 360°
Walk once around the outside of any convex polygon. At each vertex you turn through the exterior angle, and after the last vertex you are facing the way you started — one full turn, $360°$. This is why $360 \div n$ gives the exterior angle of a regular polygon and why the number of sides can be recovered from one exterior angle.

@EX | 7.5.1 | Interior angles of a hexagon
GIVEN: A regular hexagon.
ASKED: Find each interior angle and each exterior angle.
METHOD: Use the interior sum, then divide.
STEPS:
1. $n = 6$, so the interior sum is $(6 - 2) \times 180° = 720°$.
2. Each interior angle is $720° \div 6 = 120°$.
3. Each exterior angle is $180° - 120° = 60°$, which also equals $360° \div 6$.
ANSWER: $120°$ interior, $60°$ exterior.
CHECK: Six exterior angles of $60°$ make $360°$, a full turn.

@EX | 7.5.2 | Working backwards to the number of sides
GIVEN: A regular polygon whose interior angles are each $156°$.
ASKED: How many sides does it have?
METHOD: Find the exterior angle first; it is smaller and divides 360 exactly.
STEPS:
1. Exterior angle $= 180° - 156° = 24°$.
2. $n = 360° \div 24° = 15$.
3. Check: $(15 - 2) \times 180° = 2340°$, and $2340° \div 15 = 156°$.
ANSWER: 15 sides.
WATCH: Dividing 360 by the interior angle would give $2.3$, which cannot be a number of sides. The exterior angle is the one that shares the full turn.

@EX | 7.5.3 | An irregular polygon
GIVEN: A pentagon with angles $100°$, $115°$, $95°$, $130°$ and $x$.
ASKED: Find $x$.
METHOD: Interior sum for five sides is $540°$.
STEPS:
1. $100 + 115 + 95 + 130 = 440$.
2. $x = 540 - 440 = 100$.
ANSWER: $x = 100°$.
CHECK: $440 + 100 = 540$, and every angle is below $180°$, so the pentagon is convex.

@SEC | 7.6 | Constructions with compasses | Criterion C · ii, iv; Criterion D · ii
@LEAD | A construction is a drawing made with compasses and a straight edge only, and it can be justified by the geometry it relies on. Constructions are how geometry is made precise without a protractor.

@DEF | Construction
A **construction** is an accurate drawing made using only a straight edge and compasses. **Arcs** mark equal distances, and the intersections of arcs locate points exactly.
Two constructions appear in this chapter: the **perpendicular bisector** of a segment, and the **bisector of an angle**.
@METHOD | Perpendicular bisector of $AB$
@LISTO
1. Open the compasses to more than half of $AB$.
2. With the point at $A$, draw arcs above and below the segment.
3. With the same opening and the point at $B$, draw arcs to cut the first pair.
4. Draw the line through the two intersections. It is perpendicular to $AB$ and passes through its midpoint.
@METHOD | Bisector of an angle
@LISTO
1. With the point at the vertex, draw an arc cutting both arms.
2. With the point at each intersection, draw arcs of equal radius that cross inside the angle.
3. Draw the line from the vertex through the crossing point. It divides the angle into two equal parts.
@KEY | Why constructions can be trusted
Every arc is drawn with a fixed compass opening, so every point on it is the same distance from the centre. The perpendicular bisector works because its two points are each equidistant from $A$ and $B$, and the set of such points is exactly that line. Nothing is estimated; the accuracy comes from equal distances.
@TABLE | Construction and its justification
Construction ; What is drawn ; Why it works
Perpendicular bisector ; arcs of equal radius from both ends ; points equidistant from the endpoints
Angle bisector ; arcs at the vertex, then equal arcs inside ; equal distances from both arms
Equilateral triangle ; two arcs of radius equal to the base ; three equal sides
Triangle from three sides ; arcs at each end of the base ; the third vertex is fixed by two distances
@ERR | Rubbing out the construction lines
The arcs are the evidence that the construction is valid. Removing them leaves a drawing that cannot be checked. In an examination, construction arcs are usually required for full marks, and they should be drawn lightly but visibly.

@EX | 7.6.1 | Constructing a perpendicular bisector
GIVEN: A segment $AB$ of length 8 cm.
ASKED: Construct its perpendicular bisector and state the distance from $A$ to the midpoint.
METHOD: Follow the standard construction.
STEPS:
1. Set the compasses to more than 4 cm, say 6 cm.
2. Draw arcs from $A$ and from $B$, above and below.
3. Join the two intersections.
4. The bisector crosses $AB$ at 4 cm from $A$.
ANSWER: The bisector meets $AB$ at its midpoint, 4 cm from $A$, at 90°.
CHECK: Measure both halves and both angles; each pair should be equal.

@EX | 7.6.2 | Constructing a triangle from three sides
GIVEN: Sides 7 cm, 5 cm and 4 cm.
ASKED: Construct the triangle and decide whether a different triangle with the same sides is possible.
METHOD: Draw the longest side as base, then locate the third vertex with two arcs.
STEPS:
1. Check the triangle inequality: $5 + 4 = 9 > 7$, so a triangle exists.
2. Draw $AB = 7$ cm.
3. With centre $A$ and radius 5 cm draw an arc; with centre $B$ and radius 4 cm draw an arc.
4. Join the intersection to $A$ and $B$.
ANSWER: The triangle is fixed: the two arcs meet at one point above $AB$ and its mirror image below, giving the same triangle.
CHECK: Measuring the angles of both versions gives the same three angles.
WATCH: If the two shorter sides barely exceed the longest, the arcs meet at a very shallow angle and small errors in compass opening produce a visibly wrong triangle.

@EX | 7.6.3 | Using a construction to solve a problem
GIVEN: A school wants a flagpole equidistant from two gateposts $A$ and $B$, and also on the path that runs between them.
ASKED: Describe where the flagpole should stand.
METHOD: Translate the conditions into a geometric locus.
STEPS:
1. Equidistant from $A$ and $B$ means on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$.
2. On the path means on the segment $AB$.
3. The only point on both is the midpoint of $AB$.
ANSWER: At the midpoint of $AB$, found by constructing the perpendicular bisector.
CHECK: The midpoint is 4 cm from each end on a drawing of an 8 cm path.

@MOVE | G | Reflect and consolidate | Summary, vocabulary, misconceptions and review
@SEC | Review | Consolidating Chapter 7 | Criterion A · i, ii, iii
@H2 | What this chapter established
@LISTU
- Angles are measured in degrees and classified as acute, right, obtuse, straight or reflex.
- Angles on a straight line sum to 180°; angles at a point sum to 360°; vertically opposite angles are equal.
- When a transversal crosses parallel lines, corresponding and alternate angles are equal and co-interior angles sum to 180°.
- The angles of a triangle sum to 180°, and an isosceles triangle has two equal base angles.
- Three lengths form a triangle exactly when the sum of the two shorter exceeds the longest.
- Constructions with compasses produce exact figures, justified by equal distances.
@H2 | Essential vocabulary
@TABLE | Vocabulary for Chapter 7
Term | Meaning in this course
Acute | An angle between 0° and 90°.
Obtuse | An angle between 90° and 180°.
Reflex | An angle between 180° and 360°.
Vertically opposite | Angles opposite each other where two lines cross; equal.
Transversal | A line crossing two or more lines.
Corresponding angles | Angles in the same position at each crossing of parallel lines; equal.
Alternate angles | Angles on opposite sides of a transversal between parallel lines; equal.
Co-interior angles | Angles on the same side of a transversal between parallel lines; sum to 180°.
Isosceles | A triangle with two equal sides and two equal base angles.
Construction | An exact drawing made with compasses and a straight edge.
@H2 | Common misconceptions
@TABLE | Misconception, correction and evidence
Misconception ; Correction ; Evidence
“The angles of a triangle add to 360°.” ; They add to 180°. ; 60 + 60 + 60 = 180
“Alternate angles add to 180°.” ; They are equal; co-interior angles add. ; Z against C
“A diagram proves the angle.” ; Only given information and reasons do. ; Labelled 85° may look 90°
“3, 4, 7 form a triangle.” ; The sum equals the longest, so flat. ; $3 + 4 = 7$
“Constructions are approximate.” ; They are exact if the arcs are kept. ; Equal radii fix the points
