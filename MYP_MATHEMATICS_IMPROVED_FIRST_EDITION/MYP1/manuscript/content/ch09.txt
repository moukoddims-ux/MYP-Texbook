@MOVE | A | Engage | The box that holds more than it should
@LEAD | Two cereal boxes stand side by side. Box A is 20 cm by 12 cm by 6 cm. Box B is 18 cm by 14 cm by 7 cm. The manufacturer claims Box B holds more but uses less cardboard.
@P | Both claims need checking, and they are different measurements. Volume tells us how much fits inside: Box A holds $20 \\times 12 \\times 6 = 1{,}440$ cm³ and Box B holds $18 \\times 14 \\times 7 = 1{,}764$ cm³. So the first claim is true. @P | The reason is that a shape can be more compact — closer to a cube — and hold more for the same amount of material. This is not a trick of the numbers; it is a geometric principle that packaging designers use, and it is why milk cartons are close to cuboids rather than long thin boxes.

@SEC | 9.1 | Perimeter and area of rectangles and triangles | Criterion A · i, ii
@LEAD | Perimeter measures a boundary in units of length; area measures a surface in square units. Confusing them is the commonest error in this chapter, and the units are the quickest way to detect it.
@DEF | Perimeter and area
The **perimeter** of a shape is the total length of its boundary. The **area** is the amount of surface it covers, measured in square units.
For a rectangle of length $l$ and width $w$: perimeter $= 2(l + w)$ and area $= lw$.
For a triangle with base $b$ and perpendicular height $h$: area $= \\frac{1}{2}bh$.
@FIG | c09_triangle
@KEY | Base and height must be perpendicular
The height of a triangle is measured at right angles to the chosen base, which may lie outside the triangle for an obtuse triangle. Using a sloping side instead of the perpendicular height gives the wrong area — sometimes a large overestimate.
@TABLE | The formulas of this section
Shape ; Perimeter ; Area
Rectangle ; $2(l + w)$ ; $lw$
Square ; $4s$ ; $s^{2}$
Triangle ; sum of three sides ; $\\frac{1}{2}bh$
Parallelogram ; $2(a + b)$ ; $bh$ (perpendicular height)
@METHOD | Choosing and applying a formula
@LISTO
1. Identify the shape and the quantity asked for: boundary (perimeter) or surface (area).
2. Write the formula before substituting, so the units are clear.
3. Substitute with units and calculate.
4. State the answer in the correct units: cm for perimeter, cm² for area.
5. Check plausibility: area grows with the square of the length, so doubling the sides quadruples the area.
@ERR | Mixing units of length and area
A rectangle 6 cm by 4 cm has area 24 cm², not 24 cm. Writing the unit as part of the calculation — $6 \\text{ cm} \\times 4 \\text{ cm} = 24 \\text{ cm}^{2}$ — makes the difference visible and prevents the error at the end.

@EX | 9.1.1 | Rectangle and square
GIVEN: A rectangle 12 cm by 7 cm, and a square of side 9 cm.
ASKED: Find the perimeter and area of each.
METHOD: Substitute into the formulas.
STEPS:
1. Rectangle: perimeter $= 2(12 + 7) = 38$ cm; area $= 12 \\times 7 = 84$ cm².
2. Square: perimeter $= 36$ cm; area $= 81$ cm².
ANSWER: 38 cm and 84 cm²; 36 cm and 81 cm².
CHECK: The square's perimeter is smaller than the rectangle's although its area is nearly the same — a reminder that the two measures are independent.

@EX | 9.1.2 | Area of a triangle
GIVEN: A triangle with base 14 cm and perpendicular height 9 cm.
ASKED: Find its area.
METHOD: Use area $= \\frac{1}{2}bh$.
STEPS:
1. Area $= \\frac{1}{2} \\times 14 \\times 9$.
2. $= 63$.
ANSWER: 63 cm².
CHECK: The triangle is half the rectangle 14 by 9, whose area is 126 cm².

@EX | 9.1.3 | Working backwards
GIVEN: A rectangle has area 96 cm² and width 8 cm.
ASKED: Find its length and perimeter.
METHOD: Rearrange the area formula.
STEPS:
1. $l \\times 8 = 96$, so $l = 12$ cm.
2. Perimeter $= 2(12 + 8) = 40$ cm.
ANSWER: 12 cm; 40 cm.
CHECK: $12 \\times 8 = 96$.
WATCH: Dividing the area by the width gives a length, not an area. The unit check protects the answer.

@SEC | 9.2 | Area of parallelograms and compound shapes | Criterion A · ii; Criterion C · ii
@LEAD | New shapes are handled by cutting and rearranging them into shapes whose area is already known. This is the method behind every area formula in the chapter.
@DEF | Parallelogram area
For a parallelogram with base $b$ and perpendicular height $h$, area $= bh$.
The height is the perpendicular distance between the parallel sides, not the length of a sloping side.
@KEY | Why the parallelogram formula works
Cut a right-angled triangle from one end of the parallelogram and move it to the other end. The result is a rectangle with the same base and height. The area is unchanged because nothing was added or removed, only moved: $bh$.
@FIG | c09_compound
@METHOD | Area of a compound shape
@LISTO
1. Split the shape into rectangles, triangles or parallelograms, using lines you can measure.
2. Calculate the area of each part.
3. Add the parts, or subtract the parts removed from a larger shape.
4. Check that the parts do not overlap and that nothing is counted twice.
@TABLE | Two methods for one shape
Method ; Parts ; Total
Add the parts ; 10 × 4 = 40 and 4 × 6 = 24 ; 64 cm²
Subtract from a rectangle ; $10 \\times 10 = 100$ minus $6 \\times 6 = 36$ ; 64 cm²
@ERR | Using a sloping side as the height
A parallelogram has sides 8 cm and 5 cm with a perpendicular height of 4 cm. Its area is $8 \\times 4 = 32$ cm², not $8 \\times 5 = 40$ cm². The sloping side is a length of the boundary, not the distance between the parallel sides.

@EX | 9.2.1 | Area of a parallelogram
GIVEN: A parallelogram with base 11 cm and perpendicular height 7 cm.
ASKED: Find its area.
METHOD: Multiply base by perpendicular height.
STEPS:
1. Area $= 11 \\times 7$.
2. $= 77$.
ANSWER: 77 cm².
CHECK: The rectangle with the same base and height has the same area.

@EX | 9.2.2 | A compound shape by adding
GIVEN: An L-shaped room made from a 9 m by 5 m rectangle and a 4 m by 3 m rectangle joined along a side.
ASKED: Find the area and the perimeter.
METHOD: Add the rectangles; add the outer boundary only.
STEPS:
1. Area: $45 + 12 = 57$ m².
2. Perimeter: the outer boundary measures 9 + 5 + 5 + 3 + 4 + 2 = 28 m.
ANSWER: 57 m² and 28 m.
CHECK: The perimeter is not the sum of both rectangles' perimeters, because the joining edge is inside the shape.

@EX | 9.2.3 | A compound shape by subtracting
GIVEN: A square of side 12 cm with a square of side 5 cm cut from one corner.
ASKED: Find the area and the perimeter.
METHOD: Subtract the removed area; the perimeter is unchanged by a corner cut.
STEPS:
1. Area: $144 - 25 = 119$ cm².
2. Perimeter: the two removed edges replace two edges of equal length, so it is still $4 \\times 12 = 48$ cm.
ANSWER: 119 cm² and 48 cm.
CHECK: Cutting a corner from the boundary removes two segments and adds two of the same lengths.
WATCH: A corner cut leaves the perimeter the same; a cut from the middle of an edge increases it.

@SEC | 9.3 | Circles: circumference and area | Criterion A · i, ii; Criterion C · ii
@LEAD | A circle has no straight edges, so its perimeter and area are not measured by counting squares. Both come from one length, the radius, and one constant, $\pi$.
@FIG | c09_circle
@DEF | Circumference and area of a circle
The **circumference** is the distance around a circle: $C = 2\pi r$, or $C = \pi d$ because $d = 2r$.
The **area** is the space inside: $A = \pi r^{2}$.
Here $r$ is the radius, $d$ the diameter, and $\pi \approx 3.14159$, often used as $3.14$ or $\frac{22}{7}$.
@KEY | What π is
$\pi$ is the same number for every circle: the circumference divided by the diameter. Measure round any circular lid and across it, and the first divided by the second is close to 3.14 each time. Because $\pi$ never ends or repeats, any decimal answer is an approximation, so state the value of $\pi$ you used.
@METHOD | Choosing the right formula
@LISTO
1. Decide what is being measured: a length around the edge (circumference) or space inside (area).
2. Identify the radius. If the diameter is given, halve it.
3. Substitute into $C = 2\pi r$ or $A = \pi r^{2}$.
4. Give units: cm for circumference, cm² for area.
5. Round sensibly — two decimal places is usual unless the question says otherwise.
@TABLE | The same circle, three questions
Radius ; Diameter ; Circumference $2\pi r$ ; Area $\pi r^{2}$
5 cm ; 10 cm ; 31.4 cm ; 78.5 cm²
7 cm ; 14 cm ; 44.0 cm ; 153.9 cm²
2.5 m ; 5 m ; 15.7 m ; 19.6 m²
@ERR | Mixing up radius and diameter
$A = \pi r^{2}$ uses the radius. Using the diameter in the area formula multiplies the area by 4, not by 2: a circle of diameter 10 cm has area $25\pi \approx 78.5$ cm², not $100\pi$. A second common slip is squaring the wrong quantity: $(2r)^{2}$ is not $2r^{2}$.

@EX | 9.3.1 | Circumference from the diameter
GIVEN: A circular tabletop of diameter 1.2 m.
ASKED: Find the length of edging needed to go right round it.
METHOD: Use $C = \pi d$.
STEPS:
1. $C = \pi \times 1.2$.
2. $C \approx 3.14159 \times 1.2 = 3.77$ m.
ANSWER: About 3.77 m of edging.
CHECK: The circumference is a little more than three diameters: $3 \times 1.2 = 3.6$, and 3.77 is a little more.

@EX | 9.3.2 | Area from the radius
GIVEN: A circular flower bed of radius 3 m.
ASKED: Find the area to be covered with soil.
METHOD: Use $A = \pi r^{2}$.
STEPS:
1. $r^{2} = 9$.
2. $A = \pi \times 9 \approx 28.27$.
ANSWER: About 28.3 m².
CHECK: A square of side 6 m around it has area 36 m², and the circle fills a little less than that.

@EX | 9.3.3 | A composite shape with a semicircle
GIVEN: A running track end: a rectangle 20 m by 12 m with a semicircle of diameter 12 m attached to one short side.
ASKED: Find the total area and the perimeter of the outside edge.

STEPS:
1. Rectangle area $= 20 \times 12 = 240$ m².
2. Semicircle: radius 6 m, area $= \frac{1}{2}\pi \times 36 \approx 56.5$ m².

@SEC | 9.4 | Surface area of cuboids and prisms | Criterion A · ii; Criterion C · ii
@LEAD | Surface area is the total area of the faces of a solid. A net makes it visible: unfolding the solid into a flat shape turns a three-dimensional question into an area question.
@FIG | c09_net
@DEF | Surface area
The **surface area** of a solid is the sum of the areas of all its faces, measured in square units.
For a cuboid with dimensions $l$, $w$ and $h$: surface area $= 2(lw + lh + wh)$.
@KEY | Why the formula has a factor of 2
A cuboid has three pairs of opposite faces, and each pair is equal: top and bottom ($lw$), front and back ($lh$), left and right ($wh$). Adding one of each and doubling gives the total. The factor of 2 is not a rule to memorise but a consequence of the pairs.
@METHOD | Surface area of a cuboid
@LISTO
1. Identify the three different dimensions.
2. Calculate the three different face areas.
3. Add them and double the total.
4. Check the total against the net: six faces, in three equal pairs.
@TABLE | Surface area of a 6 × 4 × 3 cm cuboid
Face ; Area ; How many
6 × 4 ; 24 cm² ; 2
6 × 3 ; 18 cm² ; 2
4 × 3 ; 12 cm² ; 2
Total ; $2(24 + 18 + 12)$ ; 108 cm²
@ERR | Forgetting the hidden faces
Counting only the faces you can see in a drawing gives half the surface area. A cuboid has six faces, and the back, bottom and one side are hidden in any single view. The net is the reliable picture because nothing is hidden.

@EX | 9.4.1 | A cuboid
GIVEN: A cuboid 10 cm by 6 cm by 4 cm.
ASKED: Find its surface area.
METHOD: Use $2(lw + lh + wh)$.
STEPS:
1. $lw = 60$, $lh = 40$, $wh = 24$.
2. Sum $= 124$.
3. $2 \\times 124 = 248$.
ANSWER: 248 cm².
CHECK: The net has two 60 cm², two 40 cm² and two 24 cm² faces: $120 + 80 + 48 = 248$.

@EX | 9.4.2 | A cube
GIVEN: A cube of side 7 cm.
ASKED: Find its volume and surface area.
METHOD: Use the special cases of the cuboid formulas.
STEPS:
1. Volume $= 7^{3} = 343$ cm³.
2. Surface area $= 6 \\times 49 = 294$ cm².
ANSWER: 343 cm³ and 294 cm².
CHECK: Six equal faces of 49 cm².

@EX | 9.4.3 | A box without a lid
GIVEN: An open-topped box 20 cm by 15 cm by 10 cm.
ASKED: Find the area of card needed.

@SEC | 9.5 | Volume of cuboids and prisms | Criterion A · ii, iii
@LEAD | Volume measures the space a solid occupies, in cubic units. For a cuboid it is the product of three lengths, and for any prism it is the area of the cross-section multiplied by the length.
@FIG | c09_prism
@DEF | Volume and capacity
The **volume** of a cuboid is $l \\times w \\times h$, measured in cubic units. The volume of any **prism** is the area of its cross-section multiplied by its length.
**Capacity** is the volume of liquid or gas a container can hold, usually measured in litres: $1 \\text{ litre} = 1{,}000 \\text{ cm}^{3}$ and $1 \\text{ m}^{3} = 1{,}000 \\text{ litres}$.
@FIG | c09_units
@KEY | Units follow the dimensions
Length uses one factor of 10; area uses two, so $1 \\text{ cm}^{2} = 100 \\text{ mm}^{2}$; volume uses three, so $1 \\text{ cm}^{3} = 1{,}000 \\text{ mm}^{3}$. This is why a litre — 1,000 cm³ — is a cube about 10 cm on each side.
@METHOD | Volume and capacity problems
@LISTO
1. Identify the solid and the dimensions, converting to the same unit first.
2. Apply the formula: $lwh$ for a cuboid, cross-section × length for a prism.
3. State the volume in cubic units.
4. Convert to litres if the question asks about capacity: divide cm³ by 1,000, or m³ by 0.001.
@TABLE | Volume and capacity
Solid ; Dimensions ; Volume ; Capacity
Cuboid ; 20 × 15 × 10 cm ; 3,000 cm³ ; 3 litres
Cuboid ; 1 m × 50 cm × 40 cm ; 200,000 cm³ ; 200 litres
Cube ; 10 cm side ; 1,000 cm³ ; 1 litre
Tank ; 2 m × 1 m × 0.5 m ; 1 m³ ; 1,000 litres
@ERR | Mixing units inside one formula
$2 \\text{ m} \\times 50 \\text{ cm} \\times 40 \\text{ cm}$ must be calculated as $200 \\times 50 \\times 40$ cm³, not $2 \\times 50 \\times 40$. Converting all lengths to the same unit before multiplying prevents an error of a factor of 100.

CHECK: A litre is 1,000 cm³, so 5,400 cm³ is a little over five litres.

@EX | 9.5.2 | Volume of a prism
GIVEN: A triangular prism whose cross-section is a triangle with base 8 cm and height 5 cm; the prism is 20 cm long.
ASKED: Find its volume.

@SEC | 9.6 | Using measurement to make decisions | Criterion D · i, ii, iii, iv
@LEAD | Measurement answers questions about cost, capacity and material. The mathematics is in choosing the right measure, keeping the units consistent and stating what the answer does not cover.
@METHOD | A decision routine
@LISTO
1. Decide which quantity matters: length for fencing, area for paint or card, volume for capacity.
2. Convert all measurements to one unit before calculating.

@KEY | Cost per unit is the fair comparison
Two boxes may cost the same but hold different volumes, or cost different amounts for the same capacity. Comparing cost per litre, or per square metre, makes the comparison meaningful — the same reasoning as comparing fractions by a common denominator.
@ERR | Comparing totals instead of rates
“Pack A costs 6 for 2 litres and pack B costs 8 for 3 litres, so A is cheaper” compares totals, not value. Pack A costs 3 per litre and pack B about 2.67 per litre, so B is the better buy.

@EX | 9.6.1 | Paint for a room
GIVEN: A room has four walls, each 4 m by 2.5 m, ignoring doors and windows. One litre of paint covers 10 m² and paint is sold in 5-litre tins.
ASKED: Find the number of tins needed.
METHOD: Find the area, then the paint, then the tins, rounding up.
STEPS:
1. Area of one wall $= 10$ m²; four walls $= 40$ m².
2. Paint needed $= 40 \\div 10 = 4$ litres.
3. One 5-litre tin covers 50 m², so one tin is enough.
ANSWER: One tin.
CHECK: 5 litres covers 50 m², which exceeds 40 m².

@EX | 9.6.2 | Comparing two cartons
GIVEN: Carton A: 12 cm by 10 cm by 8 cm at 1.20 units. Carton B: 15 cm by 10 cm by 6 cm at 1.35 units.
ASKED: Decide which carton is better value.
METHOD: Find each volume and the cost per 100 cm³.
STEPS:
1. A: $12 \\times 10 \\times 8 = 960$ cm³.
2. B: $15 \\times 10 \\times 6 = 900$ cm³.
3. A costs $1.20 \\div 960 \\times 100 = 0.125$ per 100 cm³; B costs $1.35 \\div 900 \\times 100 = 0.15$ per 100 cm³.
ANSWER: Carton A is better value, despite its lower apparent capacity being irrelevant: it holds more and costs less.
CHECK: A holds 960 cm³ for 1.20 units and B holds 900 cm³ for 1.35 units.

@EX | 9.6.3 | Designing a box
GIVEN: A manufacturer wants a box of volume 1,000 cm³ with a square base, using as little card as possible.
ASKED: Choose the dimensions and justify the choice.
METHOD: Test three square-based boxes and compare surface areas.
STEPS:
1. Base 10 × 10, height 10: surface area $6 \\times 100 = 600$ cm².
2. Base 5 × 5, height 40: surface area $2(25) + 4(200) = 850$ cm².
3. Base 20 × 20, height 2.5: surface area $2(400) + 4(50) = 1{,}000$ cm².
ANSWER: The cube of side 10 cm, using 600 cm² — the least card of the three.
CHECK: All three have volume 1,000 cm³, and the cube has the smallest surface area.
WATCH: A very flat or very tall box of the same volume always uses more card. The compact shape wins.

@MOVE | G | Reflect and consolidate | Summary, vocabulary, misconceptions and review
@SEC | Review | Consolidating Chapter 9 | Criterion A · i, ii, iii
@H2 | What this chapter established
@LISTU
- Perimeter is a length; area is measured in square units; volume in cubic units.
- Rectangle area $lw$; triangle area $\\frac{1}{2}bh$; parallelogram area $bh$, using perpendicular height.
- Compound shapes are handled by adding or subtracting simple shapes.
- Surface area is the sum of all faces: $2(lw + lh + wh)$ for a cuboid, $6s^{2}$ for a cube.
- Volume of a cuboid is $lwh$; of a prism, cross-section area × length.
- $1 \\text{ litre} = 1{,}000 \\text{ cm}^{3}$; unit conversions follow the number of dimensions.
- For a fixed volume, the most compact shape uses the least material.
@H2 | Essential vocabulary
@TABLE | Vocabulary for Chapter 9
Term | Meaning in this course
Perimeter | The total length of a boundary.
Area | The surface covered, in square units.
Perpendicular height | The distance at right angles to a base.
Compound shape | A shape made from two or more simple shapes.
Surface area | The total area of all faces of a solid.
Volume | The space occupied, in cubic units.
